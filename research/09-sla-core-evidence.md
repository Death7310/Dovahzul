# Track 9: SLA core evidence and what it requires of an adult self-study app

Scope. In depth: instruction (c), feedback (b), skill and practice (d), fluency (e), reliability (g). Briefly: learner factors (f), statistical learning, extensive listening, motivation (another track). Dovahzul facts come from the repository's wiki snapshot (UESP 2026) and the Track 1 report, labelled canon, semi-canon or fan. "Inference" marks my reasoning. Effect sizes use the source's metric. Figures were checked against publisher abstracts where the abstract states them; those marked † are from my memory of the paper and need re-checking before public use.

## 1. Bottom line

- **Explain briefly, then make the form decide the meaning.** Explicit instruction beat implicit in three meta-analyses (d = 1.13 against 0.54; Norris & Ortega 2000) and roughly tied in a fourth (Kang et al. 2019); the tests mostly reward explicit knowledge. Give a short rule, then many items where the rule is the only route to the meaning.
- **Practice transfers narrowly.** Comprehension practice builds comprehension, production practice builds production (DeKeyser 1997; Shintani et al. 2013). Track reading, composing, rune reading and speaking separately.
- **Feedback at once, naming the rule, with one retry.** Feedback has a medium, durable effect (Li 2010; Lyster & Saito 2010), and a rule engine can give the explanatory kind that did well in software (Nagata 1993; Heift 2004).
- **Input must be plentiful and understood; canon cannot supply it.** The canon corpus is 2,442 running tokens (fan transcription of canon text; Track 1). Graded text written for the app is a core content cost.
- **Fluency is its own strand.** Repeat known material under time pressure, then check accuracy; measure speed and pausing (de Jong & Perfetti 2011; Thai & Boers 2016).
- **Space practice over days, mix confusable forms, and do not tune a precise gap** (Kim & Webb 2022).
- **Expect less than published effects.** The field's own scale calls d = 0.40 small, most outcomes are constrained tests within weeks, and one article in 400 is a replication (Plonsky & Oswald 2014; Marsden et al. 2018). Test late, test free production, promise little.
- **Sequence by dependency, frequency and transparency,** not a "natural order" (Goldschneider & DeKeyser 2001).

## 2. Findings by sub-question

### a. Input

That acquisition needs much understood input is undisputed. Krashen's stronger claims (input is sufficient; learned knowledge never feeds acquisition; correction does nothing) are not. Lichtman & VanPatten (2021) defend reworded versions in a narrative review, not a test. The instruction and feedback effects below count against sufficiency.

Processing instruction (PI) trains learners to use a form, not a default guess, to get meaning. It improved interpretation and production in the founding study (VanPatten & Cadierno 1993). Over 42 experiments PI was better for receptive knowledge; production-based instruction was equal for productive knowledge, and better when explanations were matched (Shintani 2015). Across 35 projects comprehension-based and production-based instruction both had large effects on both kinds of knowledge, and production-based instruction led on delayed productive tests (Shintani et al. 2013). The PI advantage depends on the comparison and the test (DeKeyser & Prieto Botana 2015).

Highlighting forms barely helps: d = 0.22 for grammar and -0.26 for comprehension (Lee & Huang 2008).

Extensive reading: d = 0.46 between groups and 0.71 pre-post (Nakanishi 2015); a later analysis adjusted a pooled 0.55 down to 0.37 for selection bias (Hamada 2020). Incidental word learning is slow: 9 to 18% of target words on immediate tests, reading and listening alike (Webb et al. 2023). Spoken input gives g = 1.05, less against a no-input control (de Vos et al. 2018). Repetition helps moderately (r = .34; Uchihara et al. 2019), so no exposure count guarantees a word. Comprehension rises steadily with the share of known words; 98% is the suggested target for unassisted reading† (Schmitt et al. 2011). Extensive reading also raised reading rate (McLean & Rouault 2017). Extensive listening has too few studies to pool.

### b. Interaction, output and corrective feedback

Task-based interaction has a large effect against little or none (d = 0.92†; Keck et al. 2006), and text chat does about as well as speech (Ziegler 2016). The mechanisms proposed are negotiation of meaning, feedback and modified output (Loewen & Sato 2018). A learner alone gets no negotiation; feedback and pushed output can be kept. Producing language makes learners notice what they cannot say (Swain & Lapkin 1995), and output followed by input beat visually enhanced input (Izumi 2002); both are small studies.

Across 33 studies oral feedback had a medium effect that lasted, with implicit feedback holding up better over time (Li 2010). In 15 classroom studies (N = 827) effects were durable, larger for prompts, which withhold the answer, than for recasts, and clearest on free-response measures (Lyster & Saito 2010). Written correction improves accuracy (Kang & Han 2015), against Truscott (1996); a Bayesian synthesis finds a durable moderate effect, with direct, indirect and metalinguistic types about equal (Brown et al. 2026; online 2023). Eighteen feedback meta-analyses disagree on size because they draw the domain differently (Plonsky & Brown 2015).

Timing: immediate oral feedback had "some advantage" over delayed on a judgment test and none on elicited imitation (Li et al. 2016), and was more facilitative in a second classroom study (Fu & Li 2022).

Software (from memory of the papers): feedback that explained the rule beat feedback that only marked the error (Nagata 1993); naming and highlighting the error led to the most corrected resubmissions (Heift 2004); a game that led learners to work out a rule beat deductive teaching (Cerezo et al. 2016).

### c. Instruction

Norris & Ortega (2000): 49† studies; instruction against control d = 0.96†; explicit 1.13, implicit 0.54. Only 16% of studies included a free-response measure, so the result mainly shows that explicit teaching produces knowledge visible on constrained tests. Later syntheses: explicit ahead for simple and complex features, including spontaneous use (Spada & Tomita 2010, 41 studies); explicit ahead in 34 studies (Goo et al. 2015); but across 54 studies (N = 5,051), g = 1.06 overall with only "a minor difference" between the two, moderated by outcome measure, proficiency, setting and intensity (Kang et al. 2019). Attention to form inside meaning-focused work ("focus on form") did about as well as a structure syllabus in Norris & Ortega (d = 1.00† and 0.93†) and now names a family of techniques (R. Ellis 2016).

Task-based teaching: d = 0.93 (Bryfonski & McKay 2019); g = 0.61 on stricter re-analysis (Xuan et al. 2025; online 2022); "not ripe yet" for pooling, since studies disagree on what a task is (Boers & Faez 2026; online 2023). Three re-examined meta-analyses show results shifting with how effects are computed and which studies are admitted (Boers et al. 2021).

The four strands (Nation 2007) give equal time to meaning-focused input, meaning-focused output, language-focused learning and fluency development: a planning framework consistent with the above, with an untested ratio (my reading).

### d. Knowledge and skill

Explicit and implicit knowledge load on separate factors (R. Ellis 2005), but timed tests once taken to measure implicit knowledge appear to measure automatised explicit knowledge (Suzuki 2017), which predicted implicit knowledge in one model (Suzuki & DeKeyser 2017a): weak support for an interface. In N. Ellis (2005) explicit knowledge directs attention and implicit tallying of usage consolidates; usage-based accounts rest on sensitivity to frequency at every level (N. Ellis 2002). Statistical learning is robust in the laboratory, but its link to natural language learning is unsettled (Frost et al. 2019). The agreed part is what a builder needs: quick, reliable use comes from much meaning-linked practice.

Skill acquisition: DeKeyser (1997) trained 61† learners on a miniature language; latency and errors fell along power curves, and gains were specific to the skill practised, as later for Mandarin tone words (Li & DeKeyser 2017). This is transfer-appropriate processing: memory is best when the test demands the processing used in study (Morris et al. 1977).

Distribution. Across 48 experiments spacing had a medium-to-large effect; longer gaps beat shorter ones only on delayed tests; equal and expanding schedules were equivalent (Kim & Webb 2022). Grammar is less tidy: a 14-day† gap beat a 3-day† gap on a long-term test (Bird 2010); massed practice was as accurate as distributed and possibly faster (Suzuki & DeKeyser 2017b); 3.5-day and 7-day gaps were about equal for children (Kasprowicz et al. 2019). An online vocabulary replication found the spacing effect with scores 10 to 20% below the laboratory original (Rogers et al. 2025). Mixing structures produced more training errors and better scores a week later (Nakata & Suzuki 2019); for Spanish past tenses interleaving helped only across two weekly sessions (Pan et al. 2019).

### e. Fluency

Segalowitz (2010) separates cognitive fluency (efficient underlying processes), utterance fluency (measurable speed, breakdown, repair) and perceived fluency. Across 22 studies perceived fluency correlated with speed and pause frequency at r = .59 to .62, pause duration at .46 and repair at .20 (Suzuki, Kormos & Uchihara 2021). Language knowledge and retrieval speed explained articulation speed far better than pausing† (de Jong et al. 2013). Automatisation shows as responses that grow more stable as well as faster, a falling coefficient of variation (Segalowitz & Segalowitz 1993).

- Repeating a talk in 4, 3 and 2 minutes: all sped up in training; only those repeating the same content kept the gain (de Jong & Perfetti 2011, 24 learners).
- Shrinking time raised fluency most, but not accuracy, because speakers reused their wording almost verbatim (Thai & Boers 2016, 20 learners).
- Blocked repetition (AAA, BBB) transferred to new tasks better than interleaved (Y. Suzuki 2021); six massed repetitions cut pauses but slowed articulation (Suzuki & Hanzawa 2022); 1-day and 7-day spacing were equal at 28 days (Kakitani & Kormos 2024).

### f. Learner factors (brief)

In 669,498† English speakers grammar-learning ability stayed high to about 17.4 years (Hartshorne et al. 2018); a reanalysis finds continuous decline for most learner types (van der Slik et al. 2022). Native-like command is rare among late starters† (Abrahamsson & Hyltenstam 2009), yet older learners are faster in the first months† (Snow & Hoefnagel-Höhle 1978). Native-likeness is moot for a language without native speakers (inference). Aptitude correlates r = .31 with grammar learning, more under explicit treatment (Li 2015); working memory about .25† (Linck et al. 2014). Motivational self-guides predict intended effort (r = .61) far better than achievement (r = .20) (Al-Hoorie 2018).

### g. The field's reliability

- **Benchmarks.** d = 0.40 small, 0.70 medium, 1.00 large between groups†; 0.60, 1.00, 1.40 pre-post† (Plonsky & Oswald 2014). Uncontrolled gains look larger than controlled ones.
- **Replication.** One per 400 articles (Marsden et al. 2018); still infrequent (McManus 2024). A multisite replication found site-specific differences (Morgan-Short et al. 2018); replications with non-university participants generally failed (Godfroid & Andringa 2023).
- **Practices.** Of 351 researchers, 94% admitted a questionable practice and 17% misconduct (Isbell et al. 2022). Surveys of 606† primary studies (Plonsky 2013) and 120 meta-analyses (Vuogan & Li 2024) document recurring design, reporting and bias-check problems.
- **Bias.** Mobile-learning studies pool to 0.72 with "obvious publication bias" and heterogeneity near 100% (Burston & Giannakou 2022).
- **Laboratory to life.** Pronunciation teaching works on monitored production and is unclear for spontaneous speech (Saito & Plonsky 2019), as with grammar. Nine learners of Turkish on Duolingo improved over a semester, gains tracking study time (Loewen et al. 2019). Fifty-four Babbel users gained vocabulary, grammar and speaking ability in 12 weeks, study time the best predictor (Loewen et al. 2020; a co-author worked for the company†). Neither had a control group†.

### h. Developmental sequences

Processability Theory holds that processing procedures emerge in an implicational order (Pienemann 1998), and the Teachability Hypothesis that instruction cannot make a learner skip a stage (Pienemann 1984). Tests are mixed: learners judged ready did not benefit more than the unready (Spada & Lightbown 1999), and concept-based instruction moved learners of Chinese past a predicted stage (Zhang & Lantolf 2015). The "natural order" of English morphemes is largely explained by perceptual salience, semantic complexity, regularity, syntactic category and frequency (Goldschneider & DeKeyser 2001) and varies with the first language (Murakami & Alexopoulou 2016). DeKeyser (2005) reduces difficulty to the form-meaning link: how novel, opaque and redundant.

Implication (inference): no stage table exists for Dovahzul. Its word order is English-like (semi-canon developer statement reported by the wiki; confirmed on canon text in Track 1) and its morphology is a handful of suffixes, so English-reading learners have most processing prerequisites. Order the syllabus by those five properties.

## 3. What this means for the app

### 3.1 Design requirements, ranked

**Strong** = several meta-analyses agree; **moderate** = one meta-analysis or several consistent primary studies; **weak** = few small studies, theory, or my inference. Thresholds without a source are project parameters to tune.

1. **Rule, then meaning-bearing practice** (strong for explicit teaching; moderate for the ordering). One construction card per grammar point: `{id, form, meaning, prerequisites[], canon_status, canon_examples[], contrast_with[]}`. A rule of about 60 words with two or three canon examples, then items where only the target form separates right from wrong, then production. Example: the wiki's plural rule (repeat the last letter, add *e*: *kel*, *kelle*; fan summary of canon usage) is first practised by matching each form to "one scroll" or "several scrolls". (Norris & Ortega 2000; Shintani 2015.)
2. **Separate mastery per skill** (moderate). `mastery[item][skill]` for read-roman, read-rune, compose, speak; schedule each alone; never infer production from recognition. (DeKeyser 1997; Shintani et al. 2013.)
3. **Rule-keyed feedback** (strong that feedback helps; moderate for the recipe). Validator and tutor share one rule engine; each rule carries `{hint, explanation, example}`. Rule error: mark the place, show the hint, allow one retry, then answer and explanation. Vocabulary error: answer at once. Accept every form the rules allow, including canon's optional plural and tense marking (Track 1). (Li 2010; Lyster & Saito 2010; Li et al. 2016; Heift 2004.)
4. **Assessment that can fail the learner** (strong as a methods finding). Three scores per construction: untimed recognition, free composition, timed use. "Learned" requires a pass at least 7 days after the last practice. (Norris & Ortega 2000; Suzuki 2017; Saito & Plonsky 2019.)
5. **Coverage-controlled reading** (moderate). Per text, compute the share of running tokens whose stem the learner has mastered. Release at 98%; tap glosses from 95 to 98%; hold back below. Bring each new word back in 8 to 10 later sentences. Expect one new word in six to ten to stick from reading alone, so pair it with deliberate study. Label authored text as non-canon. (Schmitt et al. 2011; Webb et al. 2023; Uchihara et al. 2019.)
6. **Fluency strand** (moderate). Mastered items only. (a) Timed recognition; report weekly median latency and coefficient of variation. (b) One passage read aloud or retold three times under a shrinking timer, then an untimed pass against a model, because time pressure freezes wording. (c) Same content before new content. A recording of a known text yields syllables per second and silences over about 250 ms without speech recognition (inference). (Segalowitz & Segalowitz 1993; de Jong & Perfetti 2011; Thai & Boers 2016.)
7. **Spacing and mixing** (strong for spacing; weak for any lag). Each construction needs at least two further sessions on later days; gaps of 1 to 7 days at first, wider later. First session blocked, then mixed with listed contrasts. Do not present a lag-optimising algorithm as evidence-based for grammar. (Kim & Webb 2022; Suzuki & DeKeyser 2017b; Nakata & Suzuki 2019.)
8. **Composition with a model; scripted role-play** (moderate for output with feedback; weak for scripts as a stand-in for interaction). Show a model beside each free composition. Role-play turns accept any string the grammar parses and that fills the turn's meaning slots. Say in the app that this is not conversation. (Swain & Lapkin 1995; Keck et al. 2006.)
9. **Sequencing by dependency graph** (weak to moderate). Order by prerequisite, corpus frequency, then transparency; confusable pairs get different first sessions; no readiness gates. (Goldschneider & DeKeyser 2001; Zhang & Lantolf 2015.)
10. **Four-strand budget** (weak). Keep each strand between 15 and 35% of the last seven days' minutes. (Nation 2007.)
11. **Low load, no profiling** (moderate). Short explanations, prompt visible while answering; no learning-style quiz or age paths. (Li 2015; Linck et al. 2014; Pashler et al. 2008.)
12. **Highlighting is not teaching** (moderate). (Lee & Huang 2008.)
13. **Honest progress display** (moderate). Hours and delayed-test results; no time-to-fluency claim. (Loewen et al. 2019; Loewen et al. 2020.)

### 3.2 Popular beliefs the evidence does not support

1. "Input alone is enough; grammar study and correction are useless." Instruction and feedback add medium to large gains (Norris & Ortega 2000; Li 2010).
2. "Adults should learn like children, by immersion without rules." Explicit teaching matched or beat implicit in every synthesis cited here.
3. "Adults cannot learn a language well" and "children learn faster." Decline is gradual, and older learners start faster (van der Slik et al. 2022; Snow & Hoefnagel-Höhle 1978).
4. "Teach to the learner's style." No adequate evidence that matching styles helps (Pashler et al. 2008).
5. "Correction harms or does nothing" (Truscott 1996). The meta-analyses disagree. "Correct everything" is not supported either.
6. "Grammar arrives in a fixed natural order that teaching cannot change." The order follows properties of the forms and shifts with the first language (Murakami & Alexopoulou 2016).
7. "Understanding turns into speaking by itself" and "drill makes fluent." Practice gains are skill-specific (DeKeyser 1997).
8. "Cram it." Massed and blocked practice look best in training and lose at a delay (Kim & Webb 2022; Nakata & Suzuki 2019).
9. "Highlighting grammar makes people pick it up." d = 0.22 (Lee & Huang 2008).
10. "Ten minutes a day makes you fluent." App gains are modest, scale with hours, and come from small or company-linked studies.
11. "A large published effect means a large real-world effect." See 2g.

## 4. Contested or weak evidence

- **Explicit against implicit.** Three syntheses favour explicit and one finds a minor difference; all lean on measures that reward explicit knowledge. That explicit teaching leads to automatic use is inferred from skill-acquisition studies, not shown at scale.
- **Interface.** One correlational model (Suzuki & DeKeyser 2017a).
- **Feedback type.** Prompts beat recasts in classrooms; implicit feedback lasted better in Li (2010); written types came out equal (Brown et al. 2026). The hint, retry, explain recipe is a synthesis, not a tested package, and the software studies are single, small and old.
- **Feedback timing.** Two classroom studies of oral feedback; transfer to typed self-study is inference.
- **Processing instruction.** Its advantage is confined to receptive measures and short delays.
- **Task-based teaching.** No agreed pooled effect.
- **Lag.** Results for grammar and fluency conflict, with small samples.
- **Fluency training.** Key studies have 20 to 24 learners.
- **Critical period.** One data set supports a break at 17.4 or a continuous decline, depending on the model.
- **Coverage thresholds.** The 98% figure comes from academic English reading; the 95% floor and the recurrence count are project parameters.
- **App efficacy.** Small, uncontrolled or company-linked; search results this session suggest newer Duolingo papers are company-authored too (not cited, not verified).

## 5. Gaps, and anything that was blocked

- No peer-reviewed study of teaching a constructed or fictional language by self-study software was found. Every requirement is extrapolated from natural languages, mostly English taught in classrooms to university students.
- Searches found no meta-analysis since 2022 that updates explicit against implicit instruction, extensive listening or developmental readiness.
- Nothing is known about learning a language with a few hundred attested words, no speech community and rules still being decided, or about rule-validated role-play as a substitute for interaction.
- Blocked or limited: WebFetch was unavailable, so no full texts were read; figures come from publisher abstracts through Crossref and OpenAlex, from search summaries, or from memory (†). All ten permitted searches were used. Three *Language Teaching Research* articles carry a registry year (2025 or 2026) later than their online publication (2022 or 2023) and are cited by registry year. The one "manual" reference is the wiki page; its URL comes from the repository's data files, not from a search result.
- Not done: corpus frequency ranks to seed the sequencing graph (the Track 1 data would support it).
