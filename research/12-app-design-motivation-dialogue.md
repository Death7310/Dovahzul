# Track 12: App design, motivation, and conversation practice without a partner or an AI

Scope. Sub-questions a, b, d and f are treated in depth; c, e and g briefly. Scheduling is Track 10, feedback and output theory Track 9, pronunciation Track 11, assessment without native norms Track 15. Study figures come from abstracts and search summaries; no full text could be opened (section 5).

## 1. Bottom line

- **Apps work for people who keep using them, and most stop.** Documented gains are beginner-level, over 8 to 16 weeks, in studies that are mostly company-linked or uncontrolled. Design for months of regular practice; claim no equivalence with a course.
- **Gamification buys small, fragile gains** (g about 0.25 to 0.5). Use game elements as competence feedback and story, never as pressure: no loss-framed streaks, lives, leaderboards or currency.
- **This audience arrives intrinsically motivated.** Protect that with choice, visible growth in competence, and routes into the fan community.
- **One new thing per screen, gloss attached to the word, learner-paced, nothing decorative during thinking.** Fade romanization and glosses with mastery.
- **Learner model: the Track 10 scheduler per item plus one Elo-style number per grammar rule.** Nothing deeper is justified by one learner's data on one device.
- **Levels: a project-owned ladder of can-do statements, each a task the app can check.** The CEFR is a style guide, not a scale to map onto.
- **Conversation without AI: branching, system-guided dialogue with constrained production and explicit correction is the best-evidenced design, and rules can build it.** Add shadowing, repeated monologues and validated composition, then hand learners on to people.
- **WCAG 2.2 AA is a release gate.** Runes are real text with a romanized alternative; nothing is drag-only or timed by default.

## 2. Findings by sub-question

### a. Efficacy of language apps

| Study | Design | Result | Caveat |
|---|---|---|---|
| Loewen et al. 2019 | Duolingo Turkish, one semester, n = 9 | Improvement; study time correlated moderately with gains | Tiny sample; learners reported frustration |
| Loewen et al. 2020 | Babbel Spanish, 12 weeks, about 10 minutes a day, n = 54 | Gains in grammar, vocabulary, oral ability; study time the strongest predictor | No control; Babbel co-author |
| Jiang et al. 2021 | Duolingo beginner-course completers, Spanish or French, n = 225 | ACTFL Intermediate Low reading, Novice High listening | Four of five authors at Duolingo; completers only |
| Smith et al. 2024 | Duolingo Spanish, three months, about 27 hours, n = 48 | Gains on all receptive and productive measures | No control; Duolingo co-author |
| Kessler et al. 2025 | Babbel versus Duolingo, Turkish, eight weeks, N = 59 | Both progressed; no significant difference | No untreated control |
| Kim et al. 2026 | Beginner French, 16 weeks: class, Duolingo, or both | Similar gains in all three; only tu/vous use favoured the combination | Funding and allocation not checked |
| Sudina & Plonsky 2023 | N = 287 Duolingo learners, six months, observational | Minutes correlated with written, not oral, gains; frequency of use and course progress were steadier correlates | Not an experiment |
| Rachels & Rockinson-Szapkiw 2018 | Duolingo versus class teaching, primary school, 12 weeks | No significant difference | Children |

**Attrition.** Nielson (2011) gave government employees Rosetta Stone or Tell Me More and reported "severe participant attrition" as the most striking finding. García Botero et al. (2019) logged 118 students invited to use Duolingo outside class; activity was low. Eysenbach (2005) argues that dropout is the normal case in self-directed digital programmes and should be reported as an outcome.

**Syntheses.** Of 1,786 records on commercial apps, eight studies met minimal standards (Tommerdahl et al. 2024); of 35 Duolingo studies, most examined design, not outcomes (Shortt et al. 2023). Meta-analyses of mobile-assisted language learning report g = 0.55 (Sung et al. 2015), 0.72 (Burston & Giannakou 2022) and 0.88 (Mihaylova et al. 2022), but the second kept 84 of 700 outcome studies and found obvious publication bias, the third rated every included study at high risk of bias, and Hou and Aryadoust (2021) document recurrent design weaknesses. These are upper bounds, mostly for teacher-led projects, not self-study apps.

### b. Gamification and motivation

**Meta-analyses.** Sailer and Homner (2020): g = 0.49 for cognitive, 0.36 for motivational and 0.25 for behavioural outcomes; only the cognitive effect held among rigorous studies. Bai et al. (2020) and Huang et al. (2020) report g = 0.504 and 0.464 on learning outcomes. For second languages, a systematic review found mostly positive learner reports from weak designs (Dehghanzadeh et al. 2021); Korean classroom studies of gamified English pool to g = 0.517 (11 cases, 610 participants; Lee & Baek 2023); digital games, as distinct from gamification, yield d = 0.50 between groups and 0.95 within, with entertainment games at least as effective as educational ones (Dixon et al. 2022).

**Elements.** Badges, leaderboards and performance graphs raised felt competence; avatars, story and teammates raised relatedness (Sailer et al. 2017). Points, levels and leaderboards raised output quantity without changing intrinsic motivation (Mekler et al. 2017). Leaderboards act like imposed difficult goals (Landers et al. 2017). In one 16-week course, the section with badges and a leaderboard ended with lower intrinsic motivation, satisfaction and exam scores (Hanus & Fox 2015). Over 14 weeks (N = 756) the benefit shrank after the first weeks and only partly recovered (Rodrigues et al. 2022).

**Rewards.** Across 128 experiments, rewards contingent on engaging, completing or performing reduced free-choice intrinsic motivation (d = -0.40, -0.36, -0.28); positive feedback raised it (d = 0.33) (Deci et al. 1999). Cameron et al. (2001) dispute the generality. Inference: points for turning up are engagement-contingent rewards; informational feedback is safe.

**Streaks.** Streaks do raise persistence: in six preregistered experiments (N = 4,493), paying for consecutive completions produced more work than larger flat payments (Mehr et al. 2025). In seven consumer studies, a highlighted intact streak raised later engagement and a broken one lowered it, less so when the break was repairable or not the person's fault (Silverman & Barasch 2023). Goals with a reserve of permitted misses were preferred and pursued more persistently than rigid ones (Sharif & Shu 2017). None concerns language learning. Inference: a streak counter buys attendance and makes the first missed day a quitting point; a target with built-in slack keeps most of the benefit.

**Engagement is not learning.** Performance during practice is an unreliable index of durable learning, and conditions that make practice feel fluent can impair retention (Soderstrom & Bjork 2015). Players of a music game recalled less of its vocabulary than watchers (deHaan et al. 2010).

**Self-determination theory.** Autonomy, competence and relatedness are the frame (Ryan & Deci 2000); their satisfaction predicts enjoyment of games and return to them (Ryan et al. 2006). Across 344 samples, intrinsic and identified motivation went with success, persistence and well-being; reward-and-punishment motivation went with neither performance nor persistence (Howard et al. 2021). The pattern holds for language learners (Noels et al. 2000; Al-Hoorie et al. 2025). Caution: the ideal L2 self predicts intended effort (r = .61) far better than achievement (r = .20) (Al-Hoorie 2018).

**Constructed languages.** Schreyer (2015) surveyed Na'vi learners in 2011 (about 300 responses) and describes a speech community formed online; in a later interview she says many joined as fans of the film and stayed for the community (Schreyer interview, SAPIENS). Proficient users of Klingon, Na'vi, High Valyrian and Dothraki are numerous enough for brain imaging, and process these languages in the ordinary language network (Malik-Moraleda et al. 2025). No study of Dovahzul learners was found. Inference: motives are already intrinsic, identity-based and social, so community routes matter as much as exercises.

### c. Multimedia and cognitive load (brief)

Working memory limits what a novice can combine at once (Sweller et al. 2019). Replicated principles (Mayer 2009; Mayer & Moreno 2003):

- Seductive details, attractive but irrelevant, reduce learning (Sundararajan & Adesope 2020).
- Put explanation next to the thing explained (Schroeder & Cenkci 2018).
- Narration beats on-screen text with pictures, mainly under system pacing (Ginns 2005). The exception for language learners: text with speech helps slightly (Adesope & Nesbit 2012), and captions help L2 listening and vocabulary (Montero Perez et al. 2013).
- Support that helps novices hinders the proficient, so it must fade (Kalyuga et al. 2003).

### d. Learner modelling without a server

- **Bayesian knowledge tracing**: four parameters per skill; mastery at probability 0.95 (Corbett & Anderson 1994).
- **Logistic models**: performance factors analysis counts prior successes and failures per skill (Pavlik et al. 2009); Elo keeps one number per skill and per item, updated after every answer with no fitting step (Pelánek 2016; Klinkenberg et al. 2011).
- **Deep knowledge tracing** needs large multi-learner logs (Piech et al. 2015); extended BKT matches it (Khajah et al. 2016) and logistic regression wins on small and medium datasets (Gervet et al. 2020). The domain model and data quality matter more than the model family (Pelánek 2017).
- **Reinforcement-learning sequencing** succeeds mainly when constrained by psychological models (Doroudi et al. 2019).
- **Mastery gating**: 108 controlled evaluations, about half a standard deviation (Kulik et al. 1990).
- **Target difficulty**: about 85% accuracy is optimal for a class of gradient-descent learners (Wilson et al. 2019; mathematics, not L2 data); Klinkenberg et al. (2011) ran at 75%.
- **Placement**: self-assessment correlates r = .466 with measured performance (Li & Zhang 2021): a usable prior, not a verdict.

### e. Curriculum frameworks (brief)

CEFR descriptors were scaled from teacher judgements (North & Schneider 1998); the levels are not grounded in acquisition data (Hulstijn 2007; Fulcher 2004), and the Companion Volume is criticised as policy-led (Deygers 2021). A fan precedent: the Klingon Language Certification Program has four levels, with an 80% pass mark on the three examined ones (Klingon Language Institute, web).

What canon can anchor (counts from the repository's UESP snapshot; Dovahzul text canon, English glosses mostly fan readings by wiki editors):

- 131 word walls, 92 opening with one of four formulas: *Qethsegol vahrukiv* (30), *Het nok* (26), *Nonvul Bron* (19), *Het mah* (17).
- 269 dialogue records but only about 1,000 words, median three; about 60 records reach five words. Paarthurnax (75), Alduin (37) and Odahviing (24) dominate.
- 29 listed phrases, few conversational: *Drem Yol Lok*, "greetings" (canon, Skyrim); *Pruzah wundunne*, literally "good travels" (canon, The Elder Scrolls Online; wiki gloss).

Inference: reading levels can rest on canon; conversational levels cannot, and need authored, forge-approved material labelled as such.

### f. Conversation and roleplay with no partner and no AI

**Dialogue-based software.** Bibauw et al. (2019) reviewed 343 publications and classed systems as branching, form-focused, goal-oriented or reactive. Their meta-analysis (Bibauw et al. 2022; 17 studies, k = 100) gives d of about 0.6 on L2 proficiency (0.59 in the repository abstract, 0.58 in the full text according to search) and supports form-focused and goal-oriented systems, system-guided interaction, corrective feedback and game features, most clearly for lower-proficiency learners and for vocabulary, morphosyntax and accuracy. Inference: many of those systems used speech or language technology, but the branching and form-focused types need neither.

**Scripted exemplars.** Branching pre-recorded video interlocutors (Harless et al. 1999; small military sample); simulated conversations in which learners refined requests over repeated attempts (Sydorenko et al. 2018); a scenario-based environment in which formulaic expressions were retained two weeks later (Taguchi et al. 2017); an immersive pragmatics game whose players valued explicit correction (Cornillie et al. 2012). Interactive fiction for German gave encouraging but small and mixed results (Neville et al. 2009).

**Drama and repetition.** A four-month drama programme (n = 24) beat a communicative class on fluency, less on comprehensibility, not on accent (Galante & Thomson 2017). Drama-based pedagogy shows positive effects across 47 quasi-experimental studies, mostly first-language schooling (Lee et al. 2015); no L2 drama meta-analysis was found. Rehearsal is task repetition: retelling under shrinking limits (4, 3, 2 minutes) gave fluency gains that transferred to new topics (de Jong & Perfetti 2011), and repetition brings immediate fluency gains (Lambert et al. 2017).

**Solo speaking.** Shadowing improved listening, most for weaker learners (Hamada 2016). Elicited imitation tracks proficiency well (Yan et al. 2016); using it as practice is my inference.

**Paths to people.** Text chat supports L2 development about as well as face-to-face interaction (Ziegler 2016, k = 14). Fan fiction and gaming communities sustain long-term L2 use and identity (Black 2006; Thorne et al. 2009; Sauro 2017). Learners in an online role-playing game reported more willingness to communicate than in class (Reinders & Wattana 2015; self-report). Tabletop evidence is thin: one qualitative classroom study in which pupils risked speaking more inside a game world (Torres-Rodríguez & Martínez-Granada 2022) and a conceptual mapping onto task-based teaching (Schneider 2023). The one educational live-action study found concerns science classes (Bowman & Standiford 2015).

### g. Accessibility (brief)

WCAG 2.2 became a W3C Recommendation on 5 October 2023; at level AA it adds a 24 by 24 CSS pixel minimum target size (2.5.8) and a single-pointer alternative to dragging (2.5.7) (W3C 2023). Other criteria numbered in section 3 are from my knowledge of the standard. Dyslexia fonts show no benefit (Wery & Diliberto 2017; Kuster et al. 2018); where one helped, spacing explained it (Marinus et al. 2016), and extra-large letter spacing does help (Zorzi et al. 2012). Learners with specific learning difficulties need more explicit instruction and lower memory load (Kormos 2017). Universal design for learning improves the learning process; outcome evidence is limited (Capp 2017). Dovahzul has no language code; BCP 47 permits a private-use tag such as `art-x-dovahzul` (Phillips & Davis 2009).

## 3. What this means for the app

### 3.1 Design principles and rules

1. **Rhythm, not streak** (confidence: medium). The learner sets a weekly target of 3 to 7 active days, the rest shown as spare days, and a session length (default 10 to 15 minutes). Show active days over rolling 7 and 28 days and total hours. A missed day resets nothing; nothing can be lost, repaired or bought. Reminders are opt-in and neutral. Evidence: Sudina & Plonsky 2023; Loewen et al. 2020; Silverman & Barasch 2023; Sharif & Shu 2017.
2. **Feedback informs; nothing rewards attendance** (medium-high). A mastery map per rule and per text ("40 of 131 word walls read unaided"). Badges only for can-do milestones. No points, currency, lives or leagues. Evidence: Deci et al. 1999; Sailer et al. 2017; Mekler et al. 2017; Hanus & Fox 2015.
3. **Autonomy by default** (medium). Three entry paths (reader, scribe, speaker), free navigation with prerequisites as advice, optional placement, a stated reason for every adaptive choice. Evidence: Ryan & Deci 2000; Howard et al. 2021.
4. **Story that is the syllabus** (medium). The frame (learning words of power, reading walls, addressing a dragon) is the learning task itself, never decoration beside it. Evidence: Sailer et al. 2017; Sundararajan & Adesope 2020; deHaan et al. 2010.
5. **Screen rules** (high for the principles, medium for transfer). One task per screen; at most one new word or rule per item, inside known material. Gloss in a popover anchored to the token; rune, romanization and gloss in one block. Romanization fades with mastery, with a manual override. Audio with text by default, plus a listen-first switch. No auto-advance. Feedback is one line plus a link to the rule; celebration under one second, skippable, off under reduced motion. Evidence: section 2c.
6. **Learner model** (medium).
   - Items: the Track 10 scheduler.
   - Rules: Elo per rule tag. p = 1 / (1 + exp(-(theta_rule - d_item))); after response r in {0, 1}, theta_rule += K (r - p), K = a / (1 + b n), n the attempts on that rule; start at a = 1, b = 0.05 and tune (form from Pelánek 2016). Item difficulty ships as authored constants by level and exercise type: one device cannot calibrate items.
   - New material: prefer predicted p between 0.75 and 0.90 (Klinkenberg et al. 2011; Wilson et al. 2019).
   - Rule mastered: p of at least 0.90 on production items, at least 8 attempts, successes on 3 separate days (threshold after Corbett & Anderson 1994; the day rule is inference from Track 10). Mastery advises and does not lock: Kulik et al. (1990) support gating, autonomy argues against hard locks.
   - Placement: a can-do checklist sets the prior, a 12 to 20 item staircase confirms it, the learner can override (Li & Zhang 2021).
   - Storage: `{ruleId: {theta, n, successDays[], lastSeen}}` plus an append-only response log.
   - Do not build deep knowledge tracing or reinforcement-learning sequencing (Gervet et al. 2020; Khajah et al. 2016; Doroudi et al. 2019).
7. **Levels as checkable can-do statements** (medium; largely inference). Six bands, each tied to a task and a pass rule (80% is the Klingon precedent): 0 rune-reader (transliterate every rune both ways); 1 words of power (recognise and say shout words); 2 wall-reader (read formulaic word walls); 3 listener (follow canon dragon speech unglossed); 4 scribe (compose texts that pass the validator); 5 speaker (sustain a roleplay scene, join community play, submit to the forge). Every word shown carries its status: canon, semi-canon, or fan and forge.
8. **Dialogue engine** (medium-high for the design class; untested for this language).
   - Data: scene `{id, setting, roles, nodes}`; node `{speaker, line, gloss, status, audio, expects: {mode, accept, requiredSlots, feedbackByRule}, next}`.
   - Five response modes in rising difficulty: choose a line; order tiles; fill a slot; type a line the validator checks against lexicon, grammar and the node's required slots; say the line, compare with the model, self-rate.
   - Branch on meaning as well as correctness. A wrong form gets an explicit correction naming the rule, then a retry (Bibauw et al. 2022; Cornillie et al. 2012).
   - Play each scene supported, then cued, then from memory, then with roles swapped (de Jong & Perfetti 2011; Lambert et al. 2017; Galante & Thomson 2017).
   - Build scenes on functions canon attests: greeting and parting, challenge and boast, counsel, oath, commemoration. Nearly every line must be authored, validated and labelled non-canon.
9. **Solo speaking set** (medium). Shadowing; elicited imitation with lengthening sentences; a scaled-down 4/3/2 retelling recorded locally with a self-check list. No automatic scoring (Track 11).
10. **Bridges to people** (low-medium). Export scene kits (phrase cards by function, game-master prompts, a one-page glossary) for tabletop and live-action play; a play-by-post template; correspondence prompts with validator-checked drafts exported as plain text. No in-app chat. Evidence: Ziegler 2016; Thorne et al. 2009; Reinders & Wattana 2015; Torres-Rodríguez & Martínez-Granada 2022.
11. **Accessibility gate** (high). WCAG 2.2 AA. Tile exercises work by tap and keyboard, never drag alone (2.5.7); targets at least 24 pixels, aim for 44 (2.5.8); runes are text in a font with a romanized accessible name (1.1.1); Dovahzul tagged `art-x-dovahzul` (3.1.2); correctness shown by icon and words, not colour alone (1.4.1); user-set letter and line spacing in place of a dyslexia font (1.4.12); transcripts for audio; no time limits unless switched on (2.2.1).
12. **Measure learning, not engagement** (medium). Show local measures: delayed-recall accuracy, share of production items, texts read unaided. Evidence: Soderstrom & Bjork 2015; section 2a.

### 3.2 Anti-patterns

1. Daily streak counters, streak freezes, guilt notifications.
2. Lives or hearts that lock practice after errors.
3. Leaderboards and leagues.
4. Points for volume, which let easy items be farmed.
5. Chests, currencies and other variable rewards.
6. Recognition-only exercises (multiple choice, tile tapping) counted as mastery.
7. Timed drills by default.
8. Mascots, animation or sound during problem solving; glosses in a separate panel.
9. Claims such as "N hours equals a semester".
10. An adaptive path the learner cannot inspect or override.
11. Runes as images; colour-only feedback; drag-only interaction.
12. Fan or forge words shown unlabelled beside canon.
13. A keyword chatbot that pretends to understand free text.
14. Sentence translation as the only output task.

## 4. Contested or weak evidence

- **App efficacy and mobile-learning effect sizes.** As in 2a: company-linked, short, beginner-only, inflated by publication bias; nothing follows self-study learners beyond six months.
- **Gamification and rewards.** Motivational effects are unstable and subject to novelty; Hanus and Fox is one course with two sections; undermining by rewards is disputed (Cameron et al. 2001) and mostly from laboratory tasks.
- **Streaks.** Rejecting them gives up a real persistence effect (Mehr et al. 2025); the choice rests on reward research and the cost of a break, not on learning data.
- **Thresholds.** The 85% rule is a derivation for simple learning algorithms, and lower success can serve retention (Soderstrom & Bjork 2015). The 0.75 to 0.90 band, the Elo parameters, the mastery rule and the level ladder are starting values with no empirical scaling.
- **Dialogue, drama and roleplay.** Seventeen dialogue-system studies, many with speech technology this app will lack; drama and tabletop studies are small, qualitative, self-report or first-language. Transfer to typed, rule-validated dialogue is inference.
- **Constructed-language motivation.** One survey of one community and an interview.

## 5. Gaps, and anything that was blocked

- No research on learning Dovahzul, and none on learning outcomes for any fictional language, was found.
- WebFetch is unavailable, so no full text was read; figures come from abstracts, search summaries and prior knowledge of the papers. The moderator tables of Bibauw et al. (2022) and the report on the 2011 Na'vi survey were not seen.
- thuum.org refuses automated access and was not consulted; nothing here describes its lessons or forum.
- Two leads from search are not cited because their details could not be checked: a three-level meta-analysis of dialogue-based systems for L2 speaking (ReCALL, about 2025) and a mixed-methods study of learners of English playing tabletop games online.
- The canon data has no rune shapes and no pronunciation guide, so script and listening exercises depend on Tracks 8 and 11.
- This report was finished in a second pass after an interrupted first: ten web searches in the first, eight in the second.
