# Track 12: App design, motivation, and conversation practice without a partner or an AI

Scope. Sub-questions a, b, d and f are treated in depth; c, e and g briefly. Scheduling belongs to Track 10, feedback and output theory to Track 9, pronunciation to Track 11, assessment without native norms to Track 15. Numbers quoted from studies were checked against their abstracts; full texts could not be opened in this session (see section 5).

## 1. Bottom line

- **Apps work for the people who keep using them, and most people stop.** Documented gains are beginner-level, over 8 to 16 weeks or roughly 27 to 34 hours, and most studies are company-linked, uncontrolled, or both. Design for regular practice over months and make no claim of equivalence with a course.
- **Gamification buys small, fragile gains** (g about 0.25 to 0.5), and its motivational effect does not survive strict study selection. Use game elements as competence feedback and story, never as pressure: no loss-framed streaks, no lives, no leaderboards, no currency.
- **This audience arrives intrinsically motivated.** The job is to protect that with learner choice, visible growth in competence, and routes into the fan community.
- **One new thing per screen, the gloss attached to the word, learner-paced, nothing decorative while the learner is thinking.** Fade romanization and glosses as mastery grows.
- **Learner model: the Track 10 memory scheduler per item plus one Elo-style number per grammar rule.** Nothing deeper is justified on one device with one learner's data.
- **Levels: a project-owned ladder of can-do statements, each of which is a task the app can check.** The CEFR is a style guide for writing descriptors, not a scale to map onto.
- **Conversation without AI: branching, system-guided dialogue with constrained production and explicit correction is the best-evidenced design, and a rule-based app can build exactly that.** Add shadowing, repeated monologues and rule-validated composition, then hand learners on to people: tabletop scenes, play-by-post, correspondence.
- **WCAG 2.2 AA is a release gate.** Runes are always real text with a romanized alternative; no interaction is drag-only or timed by default.

## 2. Findings by sub-question

### a. Efficacy of language apps

**What is documented.**

| Study | Design | Result | Caveat |
|---|---|---|---|
| Loewen et al. 2019 | Duolingo Turkish, one semester, n = 9 | Improvement; moderate correlation of study time with gains | Tiny sample; participants reported uneven motivation and frustration with the materials |
| Loewen et al. 2020 | Babbel Spanish, 12 weeks at about 10 minutes a day, n = 54 | Gains in grammar, vocabulary and oral ability; study time the strongest predictor | No control group; a Babbel employee is co-author |
| Jiang et al. 2021 | Duolingo learners who had finished the beginner Spanish or French course, n = 225 | ACTFL Intermediate Low reading, Novice High listening | Four of five authors at Duolingo; completers only; no speaking or writing |
| Smith et al. 2024 | Duolingo Spanish, three months, about 27 hours, n = 48 | Significant gains on all receptive and productive measures | No control group; a Duolingo researcher is co-author |
| Kessler et al. 2025 | Babbel versus Duolingo, Turkish, eight weeks, N = 59 | Both groups progressed; no significant difference | No untreated control |
| Kim et al. 2026 | Beginner French, 16 weeks: classroom, Duolingo, or both | All three improved by similar amounts; only tu/vous use favoured the combined group | Funding and allocation not checked here |
| Sudina & Plonsky 2023 | Natural experiment, N = 287 Duolingo learners, six months | Total minutes correlated with written but not oral gains; frequency of use and progress through the curriculum were more dependable correlates | Observational |
| Rachels & Rockinson-Szapkiw 2018 | Duolingo versus class teaching, primary school, 12 weeks | No significant difference in achievement or self-efficacy | Children |

**Attrition.** Nielson (2011) gave government employees Rosetta Stone or Tell Me More and reported "severe participant attrition" as the most striking finding. García Botero et al. (2019) logged 118 students invited to use Duolingo outside class; activity was low relative to what students said about self-directed study. Eysenbach (2005) argues that in self-directed digital programmes dropout is the normal case and should be reported as an outcome.

**Syntheses.** Of 1,786 records on commercial apps, eight studies met minimal standards (Tommerdahl et al. 2024). Of 35 Duolingo studies, most examined design, not outcomes (Shortt et al. 2023). Meta-analyses of mobile-assisted language learning report g = 0.55 (Sung et al. 2015), 0.72 (Burston & Giannakou 2022) and 0.88 (Mihaylova et al. 2022), but Burston and Giannakou kept only 84 of 700 outcome studies and found obvious publication bias with heterogeneity near 100%, Mihaylova et al. rated every included study at high risk of bias, and Hou and Aryadoust (2021) document recurrent weaknesses in design and reporting. The pooled figures are upper bounds, mostly for teacher-led classroom projects, not for self-study apps.

### b. Gamification and motivation

**Meta-analyses.** Sailer and Homner (2020): g = 0.49 for cognitive, 0.36 for motivational and 0.25 for behavioural outcomes; only the cognitive effect held in the subset of rigorous studies. Bai et al. (2020) report g = 0.504 and Huang et al. (2020) g = 0.464 on learning outcomes. For second languages, a systematic review found mostly positive learner reports from weak designs (Dehghanzadeh et al. 2021); digital games (not gamification) yield d = 0.50 between groups and 0.95 within groups, with entertainment games at least as effective as educational ones (Dixon et al. 2022).

**Individual elements.** Badges, leaderboards and performance graphs raised felt competence; avatars, story and teammates raised relatedness (Sailer et al. 2017). Points, levels and leaderboards raised the quantity of output without changing intrinsic motivation (Mekler et al. 2017). Leaderboards behave like imposed difficult goals (Landers et al. 2017). In one 16-week course, the section with badges and a leaderboard ended with lower intrinsic motivation, satisfaction and exam scores than the plain section (Hanus & Fox 2015). Over 14 weeks (N = 756) the benefit of gamification shrank after the first weeks and only partly recovered (Rodrigues et al. 2022).

**Rewards.** Across 128 experiments, rewards contingent on engaging, completing or performing reduced free-choice intrinsic motivation (d = -0.40, -0.36, -0.28), while positive feedback raised it (d = 0.33) (Deci et al. 1999). Cameron et al. (2001) dispute how general the effect is. Inference: points for turning up are engagement-contingent rewards; informational feedback is safe.

**Streaks.** Seven consumer studies show that a highlighted intact streak raises later engagement and a highlighted broken one lowers it, less so when the break can be repaired or was not the person's fault (Silverman & Barasch 2023). No peer-reviewed study linking streaks to language learning was found. Inference: a streak counter turns the first missed day into a quitting point.

**Engagement is not learning.** Performance during practice is an unreliable index of durable learning, and conditions that make practice feel fluent can impair retention (Soderstrom & Bjork 2015). Players of a music game recalled less of its vocabulary than people who only watched (deHaan et al. 2010).

**Self-determination theory.** Autonomy, competence and relatedness are the frame (Ryan & Deci 2000); their satisfaction predicts enjoyment of and return to games (Ryan et al. 2006). Across 344 samples, intrinsic and identified motivation went with success, persistence and well-being; guilt-driven motivation went with persistence and ill-being; reward-and-punishment motivation went with neither performance nor persistence (Howard et al. 2021). The pattern holds for language learners (Noels et al. 2000; Al-Hoorie et al. 2025). A caution: the ideal L2 self predicts intended effort (r = .61) far better than achievement (r = .20) (Al-Hoorie 2018).

**Constructed languages.** Schreyer (2015) surveyed Na'vi learners in 2011 on who learns, how and why, and describes them as a speech community formed online. In her later summary there were nearly 300 respondents from 38 countries aged 10 to 81, moved by attachment to the film and its values or by fascination with languages as such (Schreyer interview, SAPIENS). Proficient users of Klingon, Na'vi, High Valyrian and Dothraki exist in numbers sufficient for brain imaging and process these languages in the ordinary language network (Malik-Moraleda et al. 2025). No study of Dovahzul learners was found. Inference: the motives are intrinsic or identity-based already.

### c. Multimedia and cognitive load (brief)

Working memory limits what a novice can combine at once (Sweller et al. 2019). The relevant, replicated principles (Mayer 2009; Mayer & Moreno 2003):

- Remove attractive but irrelevant material: seductive details reduce learning (Sundararajan & Adesope 2020).
- Put explanation next to the thing explained (Schroeder & Cenkci 2018).
- Narration beats on-screen text with pictures, mainly under system pacing (Ginns 2005). For language learners the exception matters: text together with speech helps slightly in general (Adesope & Nesbit 2012) and captions help L2 listening and vocabulary (Montero Perez et al. 2013).
- Support that helps novices hinders the proficient, so it must fade (Kalyuga et al. 2003).

### d. Learner modelling without a server

- **Bayesian knowledge tracing**: four parameters per skill, mastery declared at a probability of 0.95 (Corbett & Anderson 1994).
- **Logistic models**: performance factors analysis counts prior successes and failures per skill (Pavlik et al. 2009); Elo keeps one number per learner skill and per item and updates after every answer with no fitting step (Pelánek 2016; Klinkenberg et al. 2011).
- **Deep knowledge tracing** needs large multi-learner logs (Piech et al. 2015). Extended BKT matches it (Khajah et al. 2016), and logistic regression with good features wins on small and medium datasets (Gervet et al. 2020). Pelánek (2017) concludes that the domain model and data quality matter more than the model family.
- **Reinforcement-learning sequencing** succeeds mainly when constrained by psychological models (Doroudi et al. 2019).
- **Mastery gating** helps: 108 controlled evaluations, about half a standard deviation (Kulik et al. 1990).
- **Target difficulty**: about 85% accuracy is optimal for a class of gradient-descent learners (Wilson et al. 2019; a mathematical result, not L2 data); Klinkenberg et al. (2011) ran at 75%.
- **Placement**: self-assessment correlates r = .466 with measured performance (Li & Zhang 2021): a usable prior, not a verdict.

### e. Curriculum frameworks (brief)

CEFR descriptors were scaled from teacher judgements (North & Schneider 1998). Critics note the levels are not grounded in acquisition data (Hulstijn 2007; Fulcher 2004), and the Companion Volume is criticised as policy-led (Deygers 2021). A fan precedent: the Klingon Language Certification Program defines four graded levels with an 80% pass mark on the first three (Klingon Language Institute, web).

What canon can anchor (counts from the repository's UESP snapshot; Dovahzul text canon, English glosses mostly fan readings by wiki editors):

- 131 word walls, 92 of which open with one of four formulas: *Qethsegol vahrukiv* (30), *Het nok* (26), *Nonvul Bron* (19), *Het mah* (17).
- 269 dialogue records, but only 993 words in all, median three words; 61 records have five words or more. Paarthurnax (75), Alduin (37) and Odahviing (24) dominate.
- 29 listed phrases, a few conversational: *Drem Yol Lok* (greeting), *Pruzah wundunne* (glossed "good travels").

Inference: reading levels can be defined on canon; conversational levels cannot, and must rest on authored and forge-approved material, labelled as such.

### f. Conversation and roleplay with no partner and no AI

**Dialogue-based software.** Bibauw et al. (2019) reviewed 343 publications and 96 systems, classed as branching, form-focused, goal-oriented or reactive. Their meta-analysis (Bibauw et al. 2022; 17 studies, k = 100) gives d = 0.59 on L2 proficiency and supports form-focused and goal-oriented systems, system-guided interaction, corrective feedback and game features, with the clearest benefits for lower-proficiency learners and for vocabulary, morphosyntax and accuracy. Many of those systems used speech recognition or language processing; the branching and form-focused types need neither.

**Scripted exemplars.** Pre-recorded video interlocutors with branching (Harless et al. 1999; small military sample, gains reported); simulated conversations in which learners refined requests over repeated attempts (Sydorenko et al. 2018); a scenario-based environment in which formulaic expressions were learned and kept two weeks later (Taguchi et al. 2017); an immersive pragmatics game whose players valued explicit correction (Cornillie et al. 2012). Interactive fiction for German gave encouraging but small and mixed results (Neville et al. 2009).

**Drama and repetition.** A four-month drama programme (n = 24) produced larger fluency gains than a communicative class, a smaller advantage in comprehensibility and none in accent (Galante & Thomson 2017). Drama-based pedagogy shows positive effects across 47 quasi-experimental studies, mostly first-language schooling (Lee et al. 2015). No L2 drama meta-analysis was found. Rehearsal is task repetition: repeating a talk under shrinking time limits (4, 3, 2 minutes) gave fluency gains that transferred to new topics (de Jong & Perfetti 2011), and repetition brings immediate fluency gains (Lambert et al. 2017).

**Solo speaking.** Shadowing improved listening, most for weaker learners (Hamada 2016). Elicited imitation tracks proficiency well (Yan et al. 2016); using it as practice is my inference.

**Paths to people.** Text chat supports L2 development about as well as face-to-face interaction (Ziegler 2016, k = 14). Fan fiction and gaming communities sustain long-term L2 use and identity (Black 2006; Thorne et al. 2009; Sauro 2017). Tabletop roleplay evidence is thin: one qualitative classroom study in which pupils risked speaking more when tasks sat inside a game world (Torres-Rodríguez & Martínez-Granada 2022) and a conceptual mapping onto task-based teaching (Schneider 2023). The educational live-action study found (Bowman & Standiford 2015) concerns science classes, not language.

### g. Accessibility (brief)

WCAG 2.2 became a W3C Recommendation in October 2023 and adds a 24 by 24 CSS pixel minimum target size at level AA (W3C 2023). Criteria cited by number in section 3 are from my knowledge of the standard, not re-read here. Special dyslexia fonts show no benefit (Wery & Diliberto 2017; Kuster et al. 2018); where one helped, spacing explained it (Marinus et al. 2016), and extra-large letter spacing does help (Zorzi et al. 2012). Learners with specific learning difficulties need more explicit instruction and lower memory load (Kormos 2017). Universal design for learning improves the learning process; evidence on outcomes is limited (Capp 2017, 18 studies). No language code exists for Dovahzul; BCP 47 permits a private-use tag such as `art-x-dovahzul` (Phillips & Davis 2009).

## 3. What this means for the app

### 3.1 Design principles and rules

1. **Rhythm, not streak** (confidence: medium). The learner sets a weekly target of 3 to 7 active days and a session length (default 10 to 15 minutes). Show active days over a rolling 7 and 28 days and total hours. A missed day resets nothing; nothing can be lost, repaired or bought. Reminders are opt-in and neutral. Evidence: Sudina & Plonsky 2023; Loewen et al. 2020; Silverman & Barasch 2023.
2. **Feedback informs; nothing rewards attendance** (medium-high). A mastery map per rule and per text ("you read 40 of 131 word walls unaided"). Badges only for can-do milestones. No points, currency, lives or leagues. Evidence: Deci et al. 1999; Sailer et al. 2017; Mekler et al. 2017; Hanus & Fox 2015.
3. **Autonomy by default** (medium). Three entry paths (reader, scribe, speaker), free navigation with prerequisites shown as advice, optional placement, and a stated reason for every adaptive choice. Evidence: Ryan & Deci 2000; Howard et al. 2021.
4. **Story that is the syllabus** (medium). The frame (learning words of power, reading walls, addressing a dragon) must be the learning task itself, never decoration beside it. Evidence: Sailer et al. 2017; Sundararajan & Adesope 2020; deHaan et al. 2010.
5. **Screen rules** (high for the principles, medium for transfer). One task per screen; at most one new word or rule per item, inside known material. Gloss in a popover anchored to the token; rune, romanization and gloss stacked in one block. Romanization fades with mastery, with a manual override. Audio with text by default, plus a listen-first switch. Learner-paced; no auto-advance. Feedback is one line plus a link to the rule; celebration under one second, skippable, off under reduced motion. Evidence: section 2c.
6. **Learner model** (medium).
   - Items: the Track 10 scheduler.
   - Rules: Elo per rule tag. p = 1 / (1 + exp(-(theta_rule - d_item))); after a response r in {0, 1}, theta_rule += K (r - p) with K = a / (1 + b n), n the attempts on that rule, starting values a = 1, b = 0.05 to be tuned (form from Pelánek 2016). Item difficulty ships as authored constants by level and exercise type, because one device cannot calibrate items.
   - Selection among new material: prefer items with predicted p between 0.75 and 0.90 (Klinkenberg et al. 2011; Wilson et al. 2019).
   - Rule mastered: p of at least 0.90 on production items, at least 8 attempts, successes on at least 3 separate days (threshold after Corbett & Anderson 1994; the day rule is my inference from Track 10).
   - Placement: a can-do checklist sets the prior; a 12 to 20 item staircase confirms it; the learner can override (Li & Zhang 2021).
   - Storage: `{ruleId: {theta, n, successDays[], lastSeen}}` plus an append-only response log.
   - Do not build deep knowledge tracing or reinforcement-learning sequencing (Gervet et al. 2020; Khajah et al. 2016; Doroudi et al. 2019).
7. **Levels as checkable can-do statements** (medium; largely inference). Six bands, each statement tied to a task and a pass rule (80% is the Klingon precedent): 0 rune-reader (transliterate every rune both ways); 1 words of power (recognise and say shout words); 2 wall-reader (read formulaic word walls); 3 listener (follow canon dragon speech without glosses); 4 scribe (compose texts that pass the rule validator); 5 speaker (sustain a roleplay scene, join community play, submit to the forge). Every word shown carries its status: canon, semi-canon, or fan and forge.
8. **Dialogue engine** (medium-high for the design class; untested for this language).
   - Data: scene `{id, setting, roles, nodes}`; node `{speaker, line, gloss, status, audio, expects: {mode, accept, requiredSlots, feedbackByRule}, next}`.
   - Five response modes in rising difficulty: choose a line; order tiles; fill a slot; type a line that the rule validator checks against the lexicon, the grammar and the node's required slots; say the line aloud, then compare with the model and self-rate.
   - Branch on meaning as well as correctness. A wrong form gets an explicit correction naming the rule, then a retry (Bibauw et al. 2022; Cornillie et al. 2012).
   - Each scene is played supported, then cued, then from memory, then with roles swapped (de Jong & Perfetti 2011; Lambert et al. 2017; Galante & Thomson 2017).
   - Build scenes on functions canon attests: greeting and parting, challenge and boast, counsel, oath, commemoration. Canon dialogue is about 1,000 words, so nearly every line must be authored, validated and labelled non-canon.
9. **Solo speaking set** (medium). Shadowing; elicited imitation with lengthening sentences; a scaled-down 4/3/2 retelling recorded locally with a self-check list. No automatic scoring (Track 11).
10. **Bridges to people** (low-medium). Export scene kits (phrase cards by function, game-master prompts, a one-page glossary) for tabletop and live-action play; a play-by-post template; correspondence prompts whose drafts are validator-checked before export as plain text. No in-app chat. Evidence: Ziegler 2016; Thorne et al. 2009; Torres-Rodríguez & Martínez-Granada 2022.
11. **Accessibility gate** (high). WCAG 2.2 AA. Every tile exercise works by tap and by keyboard, never by drag alone (2.5.7); targets at least 24 pixels, aim for 44 (2.5.8); runes are text in a font, each with a romanized accessible name (1.1.1); Dovahzul text tagged `art-x-dovahzul` (3.1.2); correctness shown by icon and words, not colour alone (1.4.1); user controls for letter and line spacing in place of a dyslexia font (1.4.12); transcripts for all audio; no time limits unless switched on (2.2.1).
12. **Measure learning, not engagement** (medium). Keep and show local measures: delayed-recall accuracy, share of production items, texts read unaided. Evidence: Soderstrom & Bjork 2015; section 2a.

### 3.2 Anti-patterns

1. Daily streak counters, streak freezes, guilt notifications.
2. Lives or hearts that lock practice after errors.
3. Leaderboards and leagues.
4. Points that reward volume, so that easy items can be farmed.
5. Chests, currencies and other variable rewards.
6. Recognition-only exercises (multiple choice, tile tapping) counted as mastery.
7. Timed drills by default.
8. Mascots, animation or sound during problem solving; glosses in a separate panel.
9. Claims such as "N hours equals a semester".
10. An opaque adaptive path the learner cannot inspect or override.
11. Runes as images; colour-only feedback; drag-only interaction.
12. Fan or forge words presented unlabelled beside canon.
13. A keyword chatbot that pretends to understand free text.
14. Isolated sentence translation as the only output task.

## 4. Contested or weak evidence

- **App efficacy.** Company-linked, mostly uncontrolled, short, beginner-only, completers over-represented. No study follows self-study learners beyond six months.
- **Meta-analytic effect sizes for mobile learning** are inflated by publication bias and weak designs, by their authors' own account.
- **Gamification.** Motivational and behavioural effects are unstable; novelty effects are documented; the streak evidence comes from consumer research; Hanus and Fox is one course with two sections.
- **Undermining by rewards** is disputed (Cameron et al. 2001) and comes mostly from laboratory tasks.
- **The 85% rule** is a derivation for simple learning algorithms. Retrieval research suggests lower success can serve retention (Soderstrom & Bjork 2015). The 0.75 to 0.90 band is a judgement.
- **CEFR-style levels** rest on perceived difficulty, and our ladder has no empirical scaling at all.
- **Dialogue systems.** Seventeen studies, many with speech technology this app will not have; transfer to typed, rule-validated dialogue is inference.
- **Drama, tabletop and live-action roleplay.** Small samples, qualitative designs, or first-language settings.
- **Constructed-language motivation.** One survey of one community and an interview summary.
- **Elo parameters and mastery thresholds** are starting values, not findings.

## 5. Gaps, and anything that was blocked

- No research on learning Dovahzul, and none on learning outcomes for any fictional language, was found.
- WebFetch is unavailable, so no full text was read. Figures come from abstracts (OpenAlex), search summaries and my prior knowledge of the papers; the moderator tables of Bibauw et al. (2022) were not seen.
- thuum.org refuses automated access and was not consulted; nothing here describes its lessons or forum.
- A second streak study I expected to find was not in Crossref and is not cited. A 2025 working paper on highlighting streaks on a student platform surfaced in a registry lookup, but its abstract was unavailable and it is not used.
- The CAST guidelines for universal design and the Council of Europe's CEFR texts were not retrieved and are cited only through peer-reviewed discussion.
- The canon data has no rune shapes and no pronunciation guide, so script and listening exercises depend on Tracks 8 and 11.
- All ten permitted web searches were used.
