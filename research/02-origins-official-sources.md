# Track 2: Creation history and the inventory of official sources

Research date: 2026-11-14. Evidence here is journalism, official publications and wikis, not scholarship; each source is rated in section 2.9. Language facts are labelled canon, semi-canon or fan. Checker result for the reference list: `found: 1, manual: 19, not_found: 3, verified: 5`.

## 1. Bottom line

- **The language was built as a game device, not as a full language, and its makers said so.** Emil Pagliarulo: "you're not developing it as an actual language. It's much more word based or hieroglyphic based" (Miller 2011). The forge is therefore extending a deliberately thin system; anything beyond word-level attestation is project design, not recovery of hidden canon.
- **Three people are documented in three roles**: Emil Pagliarulo (senior designer; language and theme-song lyrics), Todd Howard (game director; commissioned the song and explains the script concept), Adam Adamowicz (concept artist; drew the font). All three rest on direct quotations in one 2011 interview and one 2011 documentary.
- **Bethesda's own process is the model for the forge**: an internal wiki held the vocabulary list and "any new uses of the dragon language have to be checked back against this list for consistency" (Miller 2011). Pagliarulo reports the exact failure the forge must prevent: coining a second word for a concept that already had one.
- **The script is a 34-rune alphabet with 9 two-letter runes, no case and no C**, designed as claw scratches (three talons plus a dewclaw dot) and shipped as a keyboard font with the extra runes on the number keys (Miller 2011; UESP 2026b).
- **No official word count exists in any source I could trace.** In the repository data, 361 of 657 wiki headwords cite base-game text; 163 rest only on the Prima guide and 91 only on a fan-hosted list of untraced origin.
- **Tier by who wrote the text and whether it appears in play**, per attestation, not per word. A five-tier scheme is proposed in 2.6.
- **Do not ship the game's font.** It is a Bethesda asset with no licence; the only dragon-rune font found with an open licence is ComicDragonRunes (SIL OFL per its listing). Drawing an original OFL font is the safe route.
- **"Dovahzul" is a fan name.** Official text says "Dragon Language", "Dragon tongue" or "Draconic language" (UESP 2026a; UESP 2026e).

## 2. Findings

### 2.1 Who created the language and the script

Semi-canon (developer statements reported by press or in official video):

- **Emil Pagliarulo, senior designer and writer.** "The mammoth task of tackling such a project fell largely on senior designer Emil Pagliarulo" (Miller 2011, journalist's wording). Howard, in the Collector's Edition documentary: "'Emil, I want you to write this song.' That's how it started. 'Can you write a song that works in the dragon language that rhymes with the Elder Scrolls theme, when translated into English also makes sense and rhymes?'" (Bethesda Softworks 2011a). UESP adds that Pagliarulo voiced the player's shouts in the trailer and the game, citing the 2021 Bethesda article and a studio tweet (UESP 2026d); I did not see those primaries.
- **Todd Howard, game director.** Origin of the idea: the Thu'um predates Skyrim in the lore and "was never intended [as the] language of dragons", but once dragons were returning "that was a pretty quick decision" (Bethesda Softworks 2011a). He says he told the composer in 2006 that he heard the series theme "sung by a barbarian choir".
- **Adam Adamowicz, concept artist.** "One of our concept artists [Adamowicz] had the task of making a font. Make unique symbols for these letters that sound like this, using this scheme" (Howard, in Miller 2011).
- **Other designers.** "Even now, the designers at Bethesda continue to add new words", checked against the internal wiki list (Miller 2011). No names are given.
- **Music.** Jeremy Soule composed; audio director Mark Lampert describes a choir of "about 30 people" recording "three passes on each verse", giving "the sound of 90 people" (Bethesda Softworks 2011a).
- **Later authors.** ZeniMax Online Studios wrote new dragon dialogue and shout phrases for The Elder Scrolls Online; a developer answered a fan in 2019 that "Using Dov words in conversation isn't compulsory for Dragons" (ZeniMax Online Studios 2019). No source names the writers.

### 2.2 Design goals and method

- **Sound first.** Pagliarulo's stated reference was "a reading of Beowulf in Old English... What would an epic sound like? So I knew what I wanted it to sound like" (Miller 2011). The 2021 Bethesda article, as quoted by UESP and summarised in search results, adds Swedish, Danish and German as influences with Old English the strongest (Bethesda Softworks 2021; UESP 2026a). I could not open the article itself.
- **Words chosen for effect.** "You can choose the words for a concept that sound the best. The ones that feel more epic. The ones that roll together well" (Miller 2011). No source claims systematic derivation from any natural language.
- **The song constraint.** "It had to rhyme in English and the dragon language. It had to tell this epic story" (Pagliarulo, in Miller 2011). The first public dragon text was this song, on Game Informer's February 2011 cover, revealed 6 January 2011 (UESP 2026b). The full bilingual text is the in-game book *Songs of Skyrim* (canon).
- **Rules were tried and abandoned.** "We started off making specific rules for the way words would work together. So the way you would do 'king' would be the word for 'son' and the word for 'leader,' except you take off this one letter. And then we realized that it had started to collapse under its own weight" (Miller 2011). The result "abandoned tense, conjugation, and even upper and lower case letters, preferring that the context imply those ideas"; *fundein* is "unfurl" or "unfurled", *prodah* "foretell" or "foretold" (Miller 2011, journalist's summary with developer examples).
- **Shouts.** "Every shout is made up of one to three single-syllable words"; a lone *Fus* is exhaled, but with more words it is inhaled and the later words exhaled (Pagliarulo, Bethesda Softworks 2021 as quoted in UESP 2026a). The same article credits the 1984 film of *Dune* for the weaponised-voice idea.
- **Relationship to English.** UESP's sentence that the grammar is "structurally similar to English, with three exceptions" cites Game Informer, but the article as archived supports only the point about tense. The wording was drafted by a wiki editor in June 2011 (talk archive of UESP 2026a). Label: fan analysis. That the song was composed in English and then re-worded is an editor's inference on the same talk page; no developer says it.
- **Words at launch.** The only developer-era numbers: "more than 60 individual words that form up into over 20 complete shouts" (Miller 2011, ten months before release). The data holds 28 base-game shout records. Thuum.org's translator page, seen only as a search summary, puts the canon vocabulary at about 640 words (fan; site entry Thuum.org n.d. a). No official total was found.

### 2.3 The script's design rationale

- "How would the dragons write or scratch this language in the stone or on the ground? Everything is done with the three talons. You'll always see combinations of one to three scratches, and sometimes the dot, which is like the dewclaw" (Howard, in Miller 2011). Semi-canon.
- "It doesn't coincide directly with the alphabet we use in English. There are 34 unique characters" (Pagliarulo, in Miller 2011). The data agrees: 25 single-letter runes and 9 two-letter runes (aa, ah, ei, ey, ii, ir, oo, uu, ur).
- "the final font was designed to work in word processors like Microsoft Word. Many of the number keys... are co-opted within the font" (Miller 2011). UESP's template documents the mapping: 1 aa, 2 ei, 3 ii, 4 ah, 5 uu, 6 ur, 7 ir, 8 oo, 9 ey. The font also holds a C glyph and an unused "0" glyph; C appears only on *Legends* card packs, where the runes encipher English (UESP 2026b).
- UESP reports the 2021 article as saying the scratch-mark look was inspired by cuneiform (UESP 2026a). Not seen directly.
- **Digraph runes are not applied mechanically.** The Dragonstone inscription is transcribed MAHLaaN, with A and H as separate runes although an "ah" rune exists (UESP 2026b; wiki transcription, to be checked against the game texture).
- Nothing official was found on writing direction, punctuation, numerals or rune names.

## 3. What this means for the app

1. **Attestation-level provenance (high confidence).** Each sense carries a list of attestations: `{source_id, tier, studio, in_play, gloss_origin: game | official_publication | developer | wiki_editor | project, revision}`. The entry tier is the best attestation tier; gloss confidence is stored separately, because most T2 and T3 English is not official (2.6; UESP 2026e).
2. **Tiers are a function of a source table, not hand-set per word (high).** One `sources.json` maps each source to a tier. The owner changes a ruling in one place and every entry re-derives.
3. **Seed the curriculum from T1 and T2 (high).** Start from the 361 headwords citing base-game text (range 345 to 361: two citation groups may be add-on or ESO material). Show the 163 Prima-only words as "official word list, not attested in play". Hold the 91 Imperial-Library-only words at T5 until their origin is traced.
4. **Forge rule: the consistency gate (high).** Mirror the documented studio practice (Miller 2011). Reject a submission when (a) any existing entry already covers the concept, unless the submitter justifies a distinct sense, or (b) the form equals or is within edit distance 1 of an existing form after digraph normalisation.
5. **Forge rule: no opaque derivation (medium; inference).** The studio abandoned compounding-with-deletion because designers could not track it. Accept only transparent concatenative compounds whose parts are existing entries; forbid truncation rules.
6. **Forge rule: no obligatory inflection (high for canon; medium as policy).** Tense and conjugation were dropped on purpose. Any grammar the forge adds should be optional particles or affixes, flagged T5, never required for a sentence to validate.
7. **Shout and name validators (high).** Shout words are single syllables; a shout has one to three words; dragon names are three such words (UESP 2026a, citing the 2021 article and in-game dialogue).
8. **Script engine (high).** Store text in Latin transliteration; store the rune spelling per attestation; render new text with a greedy longest-match tokenizer over the nine digraphs, with a per-word override for cases like MAHLaaN. No case. Keep the C and "0" glyphs out of the language keyboard; allow C only in a labelled cipher mode.
9. **Font (high for the prohibition; medium for the plan).** Do not bundle the game font or the UESP TTF. Draw original outlines on the three-strokes-plus-dot scheme, release under SIL OFL, place runes in the Private Use Area and use OpenType ligatures for the digraphs. Whether the letter shapes themselves are protected is a legal question for the owner; this is not legal advice.
10. **Naming and honesty in lessons (medium).** Call it "the Dragon Language (fan name: Dovahzul)". The origins lesson should quote Miller 2011 directly and say plainly that the language was built word-first.
11. **Flagship text (high).** Use the Song of the Dragonborn early: it is the founding text and has an official line-by-line English in *Songs of Skyrim*.

## 4. Contested or weak evidence

- **Natural-language sources.** "Old Norse supplies over half the words" circulates on fan sites with no source. Developers name an Old English recording as a sound reference and, in 2021, Swedish, Danish and German as influences; nobody documents word-by-word borrowing.
- **The 2021 article** is ten years retrospective and known here only at second hand; the cuneiform and *Dune* claims should be re-read in the original.
- **UESP grammar prose** is attributed to Game Informer beyond what the article supports (2.2).
- **UESP's interview page dates the Game Informer piece "June 2012"**; the URL and the wiki's own alphabet page give 20 January 2011. The alphabet page also says there is "a symbol for every letter in the Latin alphabet" while listing 25 letters and no C.
- **"Official Bethesda Dragon dictionary"** is UESP's label for a fan-hosted list (Imperial Library n.d.). A leaked or released copy of the internal wiki list is plausible (my inference) but untraced.
- **Font tags on UESP** (CC BY-SA, Public Domain) do not match the files' actual status.
- **Rune count.** Developers say 34; the font has 36 glyphs.
- **The checker's "found" on Adams (2011)** points to a journal review of the book, not the book; I left the DOI empty.
