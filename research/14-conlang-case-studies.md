# Track 14: Constructed languages that people actually learned

Scope: Klingon, Na'vi, Dothraki and High Valyrian, Tolkien's Elvish, Esperanto, Toki Pona, Lojban. Treated in depth: authority over growth, what a small canon needs before conversation, speaker numbers, failure modes. Treated briefly: the propaedeutic studies, constructed languages in linguistics teaching, song and roleplay (little scholarship exists). Book-length sources (Okrent 2009, Garvía 2015, Peterson 2015, Adams 2011) were used from prior knowledge of them, not re-read in this session; specifics recalled that way are marked "recalled". The report was written in two passes: the first was interrupted after a full draft; the second re-checked the canon-data claims against the repository data, corrected one of them, and added sources.

## 1. Bottom line

- **Four things were present in every case that produced conversational speakers** (Esperanto, Klingon, Toki Pona, Na'vi): one written reference grammar and dictionary; a known final authority with a source-tagged canon; productive word formation so a small root stock goes far; and a standing place to use the language daily. Tolkien's Elvish lacks the first two and has produced scholars and writers of short texts, not a documented conversational community (Hostetter 2006, 2007).
- **Expect a funnel of three to four orders of magnitude.** Duolingo's Klingon course had over 400,000 starts by 2024 (Science News n.d.); estimates of fluent Klingon speakers run from 20 to 30 (Okrent 2009) to 60 to 100 (teachers' own guesses on the institute's mailing list; tlhIngan-Hol list n.d.). High Valyrian shows 514,000 to 740,000 "active learners" (Duolingo n.d.; Marketplace 2019) and no documented fluent community.
- **The governance pattern that lasted is fixed core, growth through use, ratification afterwards.** Esperanto's untouchable *Fundamento* plus an academy that makes words official after they are in use (Fiedler 2006; Garvía 2015); Na'vi's organised community proposals decided by the creator (Schreyer 2011); Toki Pona's yearly usage survey, which sorts words by the share of respondents who use them (sona pona n.d.; Track 6).
- **The patterns that failed:** a creator asserting ownership against the users (Volapük; Loglan, which ended in a schism and a trademark case), open-ended reform (Ido), freezing a design that was not finished (Lojban 1997 to 2002), and no authority at all (Elvish).
- **Canon Dovahzul cannot yet support conversation.** Of 59 core conversational meanings probed in the 657-entry dictionary, 13 have no entry at all (*what, where, why, how, if, because, two, three, help, please, drink, name, today*) and 4 have only a near-equivalent (*say, go, thank, can*). The forge's first job is a closed "conversation kit".
- **A fan standard already exists.** Thuum.org's print dictionary reports over 11,500 entries with over 5,000 Dovahzul words, a community-invented lexicon added to the game's; its learning guide separates canon from non-canon rules, and the site says it hopes to become the authoritative source (fan; search snippets only; Thuum.org dictionary n.d.; Thuum.org site n.d.). The forge starts in the position of the Lojban group or the Neo-Elvish writers: its legitimacy will come from transparency and use, not from canon status (inference).
- **No peer-reviewed study measures proficiency or time to fluency for any fictional language.** The app should define fluency operationally and state its time estimates as estimates.
- **What carries learners past a course is use:** translation projects, correspondence, regular meetings, song and verse, and visible skill ranks. An offline app can supply the texts, the tasks, the ranks and a file format for exchange; it cannot supply partners. Courses on commercial platforms brought enrolment, not speakers, and are outside the community's control.

## 2. Findings

### 2.1 How each language grew from its core

| Language | Starting core | Growth and authority | Outcome |
|---|---|---|---|
| Esperanto (1887) | A booklet: 16 rules and roughly 900 roots (recalled; Garvía 2015; Okrent 2009) | Affixes, compounding, international roots. Zamenhof gave up ownership; the 1905 *Fundamento* is fixed; the academy ratifies additions after use (Fiedler 2006; Blanke 2009) | The only one with native speakers (Bergen 2001; Fiedler 2012); about two million users by one contested estimate (Wandel 2015) |
| Klingon (1984 to 1985) | Film lines, then a dictionary of roughly 1,500 words (recalled) with a full grammar sketch (Okrand 1985) | Only the creator's words count. The Klingon Language Institute (1992) curates the canon, runs a journal, a yearly meeting and graded certification (Okrent 2009; Okrand et al. 2011) | 20 to 30 fluent in 2009 (Okrent 2009) |
| Na'vi (2009) | Film dialogue; fans reconstructed the grammar before any guide existed | Fans built the site, lessons and dictionary; the creator then engaged and decides organised community word proposals (Schreyer 2011, 2015) | About 100 speakers by the creator's estimate, about 2,000 words (Nautilus n.d.; other pages seen in search give higher word counts) |
| Dothraki, High Valyrian (2009 on) | A few words and names in the novels (Peterson 2015) | Creator hired by the studio is sole authority; the studio owns the work. One book course for Dothraki; a Duolingo course for High Valyrian from 2017, revised twice (Duolingo n.d.) | Very large app enrolment; no documented fluent community |
| Tolkien's Elvish | Decades of private, repeatedly revised drafts; no finished grammar or dictionary (Weiner and Marshall 2011) | Published piecemeal after 1973 by an editorial group; nobody may extend it. "Neo-Elvish" reconstructions are disputed (Hostetter 2006, 2007; Eldamo n.d.) | Scholarship, calligraphy, short compositions |
| Toki Pona (2001) | About 120 words, a complete tiny grammar (Lang 2014) | Creator fixed the core in a book, then the community ratified further words by measured usage (Track 6) | "Tens of thousands of members, thousands conversationally proficient", from community data (Huang and Joo 2025); 165 self-rated fluent or advanced among 1,000 census respondents in 2021 (Toki Pona census 2021) |
| Lojban (1987) | Rebuilt from Loglan after its inventor claimed ownership | Formal, machine-checkable grammar; baseline declared in 1997 with a freeze of more than five years, rolled back in 2002 in favour of a committee with a limited mandate (Logical Language Group 1997; Linguifex mirror n.d.) | A handful of fluent users (Okrent 2009) |

Two regularities follow. First, starting size did not predict success: Toki Pona began with 120 words and Klingon with about 1,500, while Elvish has thousands of attested forms and no speakers. What the successful cores shared was **completeness at small scale**: every one shipped pronouns, question words, negation, numerals, clause linking and a way to build new words. Second, every one of them was a deliberate teaching document, not a lexicon harvested from fiction. Dovahzul's record is the second kind (canon text; glosses mostly by wiki editors, per the data README).

### 2.2 Who had authority, and how it worked out

**Creator as oracle (Klingon, Na'vi, Dothraki, High Valyrian).** Coherent, but a bottleneck. Klingon speakers accept only Okrand's words, released a few at a time, so everyday gaps persisted for years (Okrent 2009). The model also depends on a rights holder's tolerance and the creator's continued interest. Na'vi improved it: the community organises and filters proposals, and the creator accepts, edits or rejects (Schreyer 2011). Schreyer (2021) frames the creator's ongoing presence as what turns an audience into a community of speakers.

**Foundation plus academy (Esperanto).** Zamenhof declined ownership, and the community made a short foundational text untouchable in 1905. New roots enter through use and are made official later. Garvía (2015) attributes Esperanto's survival against Volapük and Ido largely to this: Volapük collapsed when its inventor insisted on a veto against a reforming academy; Ido, born of a 1907 reform split, kept reforming and never stabilised. Fiedler (2006) describes the standard as kept by speakers' own correcting and discussion, not by enforcement. Fiedler (2012) shows that native speakers hold no special authority over the norm: proficient second-language users do. That is direct evidence that a language without native speakers can hold a stable norm.

**Usage ballot (Toki Pona).** The creator published a core book and stepped back; later words were ratified by surveyed usage. The community survey (868 responses in 2023) asks "do you use this word" and sorts words into usage categories, so a cutoff can be set as a share of users (sona pona n.d.). Huang and Joo (2025, preprint) find usage-driven change over time in community corpora, as in natural languages.

**Formal baseline (Lojban).** The group promised stability so learners could invest, but froze a design whose lexicon documentation was incomplete; the freeze "compounded this difficulty" and was withdrawn (Linguifex mirror n.d.; a low-grade source). A parser that decides grammaticality did not by itself produce speakers.

**No authority (Elvish).** With the creator dead and the drafts inconsistent, two camps formed: scholars who study Tolkien's own texts, and users who regularise and extend them. Hostetter (2006) argues the latter are not Tolkien's languages. Nothing can settle the dispute, because no one can rule.

### 2.3 Materials and the route to competence

Klingon learners had a dictionary, audio courses, a mailing list, a yearly gathering and a certification ladder whose first level tests about 500 words and basic grammar (recalled; Okrent 2009), and since 2018 a Duolingo course. Na'vi learners had almost nothing official and built their own, which Schreyer (2015) documents through a 2011 survey (nearly 300 responses from 38 countries per Science News n.d.) of who learns, how and why. Esperanto has had textbooks, correspondence courses, web courses and Duolingo; Charters (2015) reports that learners reach usable proficiency relatively quickly and can self-study, and Fians (2021) and Gobbo (2021) describe the digital wave feeding in-person events. Esperanto also has examinations aligned to the European framework at levels B1 to C1 since 2008, sat by more than 1,500 people by 2016 (Esperanto-USA 2016). Toki Pona is learned from one book or free web courses and then by immersion in chat; two small classroom reports exist, one finding that classes may have helped some students' circumlocution, with inconclusive results (Coluzzi 2022), and one exploratory university club with a questionnaire (Kitano 2024).

The recurring route is: a short course, then sustained written interaction, then translation or composition projects, then speech at events. I found no case where an app alone produced speakers (inference from the above; nobody has studied it). A course on a commercial platform is also not community property: the Klingon course was built by volunteers, the platform ended its volunteer programme in 2021 (recalled), the course authors' notes were later removed (Klingon Language Institute n.d.), and no official statement of either fictional course's status for 2025 to 2026 could be found.

### 2.5 Events, correspondence, translation, song, roleplay

Charters (2015) states that Esperanto became a living language "in part by teaching the language to others, in part by translation and literary creation, and in part by forming a community of users". Translation does double duty: it creates reading matter and exposes gaps. Klingonists translated Shakespeare and staged opera; Okrent (2009) found the fluent core to be language hobbyists first, with skill at the yearly meeting carrying status. Schreyer (2015) concludes that Na'vi speakers are "a digital fandom as well as a speech community" developing shared norms through use. Scholarship on roleplay specifically is absent.

### 2.6 The scholarship and its quality

The field is small and dominated by Esperanto: a bibliometric scan of over 2,300 Scopus items from 1927 to 2022 found Esperanto "by far the most documented" (Gonzalez 2024, preprint). Schreyer (2021) and Goodall (2023) are the review articles. *Reading Fictional Languages* (Noletto et al. 2023) is stylistics, not learner research. The one recent peer-reviewed empirical study found on these languages measures how non-speakers perceive the sounds and spelling of Esperanto, Klingon and Toki Pona, and calls itself preliminary (Coluzzi 2025); it does not measure learning.

**Propaedeutic studies.** The old claim is that a year of Esperanto speeds later language learning. The classic studies were small, single-school, non-randomised and often run by the teacher involved (Halloran 1952; Williams 1965). The best modern work is from Essex: Esperanto with explicit form-focused teaching raised metalinguistic awareness, but later French attainment was similar across groups (University of Essex n.d.); Roehr-Brackin and Tellier (2018) conclude that Esperanto's apparent advantages "did not translate into measurably greater benefits" and could not compensate for low aptitude, reversing an earlier levelling suggestion (Tellier and Roehr-Brackin 2013). In 111 children aged 8 to 9, aptitude and metalinguistic awareness predicted achievement (Roehr-Brackin and Tellier 2019). Verdict: not established. Do not market Dovahzul as a springboard to other languages.

**Teaching linguistics.** Sanders (2016) and Punske et al. (2020) document conlang construction as an effective vehicle for teaching linguistic analysis; this supports the forge's educational value, with evidence that is mostly course reports.

### 2.7 Lessons

- **Learnable to fluency** means complete at small scale, regular, documented in one place, and used. Regularity matters less than completeness and community (inference: Klingon is deliberately irregular in places and still has speakers).
- **A tiny canon needs** a closed set of function words and discourse formulas before it needs more nouns. The canon has pronouns, *yes, no, and, or, but, not, who, when, this, that, here, there, now*, a greeting phrase (*Drem Yol Lok*) and a parting phrase (*Drem Wah Hi*), but 13 of the meanings listed above have no entry, and four have only a near-equivalent: *Tinvaak* "talk, speech" and *Fun* "told" for *say*; *Bo* "flow, fly, move" for *go*; *Kogaan* "blessings (thanks)" for *thank*; *Aal* "may" for *can* (the last from the strategy guide, so semi-canon). *Kolos* "in which" is the only place word of the *where* kind. A wider probe in the second pass also found no gloss for *ask, answer, question, mean, understand, need, must, again, language* (canon dictionary as recorded by the wiki; whole-word gloss matching, so meanings filed under other wording may be missed). The wiki grammar notes state a plural rule, heavy compounding and head-initial order (semi-canon: wiki summary citing a magazine feature); compounding is the growth mechanism the canon itself licenses.
- **Canon against growth:** never alter the core; label every item's source; let use precede ratification; release in dated batches.
- **Failure modes:** schism (Volapük, Ido, Loglan), stagnation by bottleneck (Klingon gaps) or by premature freeze (Lojban), creator silence (Elvish; Dovahzul's publisher has not extended the language), legal conflict. On law: the *Loglan* trademark was cancelled as generic (Loglan Institute v. Logical Language Group 1992; reporter citation recalled, holding seen in secondary commentary), and whether a constructed language can be copyrighted was raised but not decided in the 2016 *Axanar* case (The World 2016; Adelman 2014 [UNVERIFIED by registry]).
- **Expectations:** years, not months, for conversational Klingon, in a community of dozens; weeks to months reported for Toki Pona, whose whole lexicon is smaller than a Klingon beginner test (community reports, unmeasured; the census pages seen give no time-to-proficiency figure).

## 3. What this means for the app

1. **A written constitution in layers (high confidence; Esperanto, Klingon).** Layer 0: game-attested text, immutable, never overridden by any forge rule. Layer 1: owner-ratified. Layer 2: proposed and on trial. Layer 3: local. Every word and rule stores `source`, `layer`, `ratified_in_release`, `ratified_by`, `rationale`. This is Track 6's tier scheme; the case studies confirm it.
2. **Use before ratification (medium; Esperanto, Toki Pona).** A proposal becomes eligible for ratification only with attestations: suggested default at least 5 sentences from at least 3 distinct contributor keys, all passing the validators. The numbers are my proposal, not from a source; make them owner-set parameters. Where usage data from several users can be pooled, report each word's share of users, as the Toki Pona survey does, and let the owner set the cutoff.
3. **Dated, numbered releases with a decision log (medium; Klingon, Lojban).** Suggested cadence quarterly. Ratified items may be deprecated, never deleted or given a new meaning. Mark each rule `stable` or `provisional`; freeze only what is fully documented.
4. **Conversation kit first (high; canon data plus all cases).** Release 1 of the forge is a closed list: the 13 missing items, a ruling on the four near-equivalents (accept the canon word in the wider sense, or coin a new one), numerals, question words, clause linkers, politeness and repair formulas ("say again", "what does X mean", "I do not understand"). Gate the app's conversation and roleplay modes on kit completeness, computed from the lexicon.
5. **Validators are gatekeepers, the owner is the judge (medium to high; Lojban, Na'vi).** Rule checks (phonotactics, morphology, collision, layer rules) decide what may be proposed; they never ratify. Publish reasons for rejections.
6. **A rank ladder tied to checkable tasks (medium; Klingon certification).** Suggested: Rank 1, 250 most frequent canon words plus the runes; Rank 2, the whole canon lexicon and unaided reading of the word walls; Rank 3, composition using ratified forge words; Rank 4, timed translation of unseen graded text. All scored by rule (cloze, form matching, rune transcription).
7. **Translation and composition as the engine (medium to high; Esperanto, Klingon).** Ship graded source texts. When a learner marks a meaning as missing, generate a pre-filled proposal with the sentence as its first attestation. Gap reports are the forge's demand signal.
8. **Community without a server (medium).** A portable, versioned text bundle (letters, verse, scenario scripts) that embeds the rule-set version and lexicon release, so any reader's app can validate it. Printable kits for a language-only hour and for roleplay scenes limited to ratified vocabulary. Use the six canon songs as memorisation and performance material.
9. **Schism management (medium; Volapük, Ido, Loglan, Elvish).** Allow labelled dialect packs, including one aligned to the existing fan dictionary if its terms allow (unknown: the site could not be read), and never merge them silently. Give the forge variety its own name so it does not claim to be "the" language. Write down who inherits authority if the owner steps away.
10. **Legal hygiene (medium; not legal advice).** Ship no game audio, fonts or art; state that the project is unofficial; license forge-created words openly so no later party can enclose them, which was the trigger for the Loglan split. Keep every lesson, word list and rule in the repository in plain files, so the course cannot be withdrawn or frozen by a host, as happened to parts of the Klingon course.
11. **Honest expectations in the interface (low to medium for the numbers).** Define three targets: read all canon unaided; compose within the ratified lexicon; converse within the conversation kit. With a 657-entry lexicon the first is plausibly tens of hours (inference; see Tracks 10 and 15). Show the funnel figures above rather than promising fluency.

## 4. Contested or weak evidence

- Every speaker count is an estimate, a self-report or a company metric. Wandel (2015) appeared in a special issue edited within the Esperanto movement, as did Charters (2015); treat their optimism with care.
- Huang and Joo (2025) and Gonzalez (2024) are preprints. The Toki Pona census is self-selected.
- The propaedeutic effect is unsupported by the strongest recent studies; earlier positive reports are methodologically weak.
- Figures marked "recalled" (Esperanto's initial root count, Klingon's 1985 vocabulary size, the 500-word first certification level) come from memory of Okrent (2009) and Garvía (2015) and were not rechecked. Na'vi's vocabulary size rests on one press article.
- Lojban's 2002 reversal rests on a wiki mirror. The 60 to 100 Klingon figure is mailing-list opinion. Thuum.org's figures and aims come from search snippets, not from the site.
- Coluzzi (2022) and Kitano (2024) are small, uncontrolled classroom reports, known here from abstracts only.
- The parallel between the project owner and Esperanto's academy is my inference; an owner without creator or canon status has no exact precedent among these cases.

## 5. Gaps and blocked

- WebFetch was unavailable; thuum.org was not accessed, so its licence, membership and governance are unknown. Only search snippets were used.
- No peer-reviewed measurement of proficiency, or of time to fluency, exists for Klingon, Na'vi, Dothraki, High Valyrian or Elvish, as far as ten searches could find. No scholarship on the Dovahzul community was found.
- Not read: two theses on Klingon speakers known from the literature, and four theses that surfaced in search (a Finnish one on Klingon speakers, an Oxford one on a conlanging community, a 2025 Colorado College one in linguistic anthropology, and one more from 2024).
- Dothraki vocabulary sizes, Duolingo's Esperanto enrolment and the current status of the Klingon and High Valyrian courses were not verified. No controlled study of hours needed to learn Esperanto, and no pass counts for its examinations, were found.
- Two book chapters, one essay and one law-review article are not indexed in Crossref and are tagged below.
- The first pass used ten web searches; the completion pass used eight more.
