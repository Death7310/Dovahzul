# Track 15: Teaching a language with no native speakers and very little text

Scope: sub-questions a, b, c and the curriculum architecture are treated in depth. Sub-questions d and e are brief, because Track 9 already covers developmental sequences, spacing and fluency measurement. All Dovahzul counts below were computed for this report from `research/data/canon-corpus.json` and `canon-lexicon.json`; the dragon text is canon (game text as transcribed by the wiki; UESP contributors 2026), the English glosses are mostly fan readings.

## 1. Bottom line

- **People do become real users of languages with no native speakers, and the documented cases share three things:** a fixed reference corpus, a named authority that rules on gaps, and learners who are turned into producers early (Hinton 2011; Amery 2016; Fellman 1973; Fiedler 2006). The forge with an owner as final authority fits that pattern.
- **Open with formulas, not paradigms.** Kaurna was reintroduced through stand-alone phrases that grow into longer utterances (Amery 2016), and classroom learners later unpacked memorised chunks into rules (Myles et al. 1998).
- **Canon is the destination, not the diet.** The running canon text is 2,590 tokens and 746 word forms; 47% of forms occur once, and 98% coverage of canon needs 695 of the 746. Graded text has to be written and generated for the app.
- **Lint every text for coverage: 98% known words unglossed, 95% with glosses, both as soft targets.** The 98% figure rests on one small study; a 2025 replication found no reliable difference between 90%, 95% and 98% (Hu & Nation 2000; Schmitt et al. 2011; Webb et al. 2025).
- **Engineer repetition.** Plan 10 to 12 meetings of each new word across several texts, plus spaced retrieval. Reading one text teaches few words (Waring & Takaki 2003; Webb 2007; Uchihara et al. 2019, r = .34).
- **No controlled study shows the nature method or comprehensible-input teaching beating grammar-translation for Latin.** Use target-language reading because of the general input evidence in Track 9, and keep short explicit rules.
- **Rule-generated text is unproven as reading input.** No outcome study was found. Use generation for sentence practice and question sets; use hand-written, machine-linted stories for extensive reading.
- **Define "fluent" by criterion and speed against the ruled corpus, never against a native norm** (Cook 1999; Brown & Hudson 2002). Self-assessment tracks performance only moderately (r = .466; Li & Zhang 2021).

## 2. Findings by sub-question

### a. Reclamation from documentation alone

**The cases.** Hinton (2001) coined the working picture: a "sleeping" language survives in notes, texts and recordings, and can be relearned by people who study that record. Wampanoag, unspoken for over a century, is being relearned from a seventeenth-century Bible and native-written documents, with holes in the record filled by comparison with related Algonquian languages (Ash et al. 2001; recalled from the chapter, text not retrieved). Myaamia fell silent in the 1960s and came back into family and community use from legacy documentation (Leonard 2011, 2017). Kaurna was rebuilt from nineteenth-century missionary records (Amery 2001, 2016). Mutsun, with no fluent speaker since 1930, is being relearned from field notes by a community scattered across a wide area, which makes its problem close to an online community's (Warner et al. 2009; recalled, no abstract in the registry). Manx kept continuity through recordings and the learners of the last traditional speakers, and its new speakers treat that record as the measure of authenticity (Ó hIfearnáin 2015). Cornish shows the cost of not settling norms: rival spelling systems divided a small community for decades before a standard written form was agreed (Sayers 2012).

**Hebrew** is the only case that produced a mass native-speaking community. The decisive step was schools that taught "Hebrew in Hebrew" to children in new settlements; Nahir (1998) describes four steps between about 1890 and 1916: attitudes, a spoken model at school, Hebrew as a second language outside school, then Hebrew as the home language of the next generation. Vocabulary gaps were filled by a language committee and by individuals, drawing first on older Hebrew roots and word patterns (Fellman 1973). Spolsky (1995) attributes the success to ideological commitment and to social conditions that other revivals lack. Zuckermann argues that the result is a hybrid shaped by the revivers' mother tongues and that such mixing is unavoidable in any revival (Zuckermann & Walsh 2011; Zuckermann 2020); the "hybrid" label is disputed among Hebraists.

**How gaps were filled.** The same ladder recurs: (1) reuse an attested word in an extended sense; (2) derive or compound from attested roots with the language's own affixes; (3) reconstruct from related languages; (4) translate a foreign compound piece by piece; (5) borrow. Kaurna and Hebrew both gave the decision to a committee (Amery 2001, 2016; Fellman 1973). Constructed-language communities work the same way: Schreyer (2011) describes Klingon and Na'vi fans building dictionaries, lessons and forums online, with the languages' creators ruling on correctness (recalled; no abstract in the registry). Esperanto has had no native norm for over a century; its stability rests on an untouchable foundation text, an academy, and self-regulation through published usage (Fiedler 2006).

**What teaching methods worked.** The literature is descriptive. Hinton (2011) argues that revitalisation teaching differs from foreign-language teaching because learners must become the speech community and its teachers. Amery's "formulaic method" starts with phrases that stand alone (greetings, leave-takings, commands) and combines them into longer utterances (Amery 2016). McIvor (2020) notes how little this field and second-language research have exchanged. Outcome measurement is rare; Peter and Hirata-Edds (2006) is an exception, using purpose-built assessment to redirect teaching in Cherokee immersion (a language that still has speakers). That proficiency in fictional languages is real is shown by an fMRI study of proficient users of Esperanto (n = 19), Klingon (10), Na'vi (9), High Valyrian (3) and Dothraki (3), whose comprehension engaged the same brain network as natural languages (Malik-Moraleda et al. 2025). It says nothing about how they learned.

### b. Active teaching of Latin and Greek

**The method.** Ørberg's *Familia Romana* teaches entirely in Latin through 35 chapters of connected narrative, with each new word made clear by context, pictures and marginal notes; the publisher gives its vocabulary as almost 1,800 words (Ørberg 1991; figure from a bookseller listing, not an independent count). Wingate (2013) traces the natural method's history and rationale. Coffee (2012) and the case studies in Lloyd and Hunt (2021) and Gruber-Miller (2006) describe spoken and communicative practice in schools and universities.

**The evidence.** One search for controlled comparisons of comprehensible-input teaching and grammar-translation in Latin found none. What exists is practitioner research: Venditti (2021) reports one teacher's class with no comparison group, and Moran (2022) is a critical essay asking whether such teaching transfers to reading original authors. Lichtman and VanPatten (2021) defend the core of Krashen's ideas in updated form, and Lichtman (2018) reviews story-based teaching research, which is mostly small classroom studies. Laboratory work with Latin points the other way from a pure-input position: Stafford et al. (2012) taught 65 Latin-naive adults by computer with task-essential comprehension practice and feedback, with or without a prior grammar explanation; the advantage lay with more explicit feedback during practice (result recalled; the retrieved abstract was cut off before the results). That agrees with Track 9.

**Two warnings from classics.** Owens (2016) documents non-classical usage spreading through active-Latin materials and teachers' speech. Piazza (2017) observes that Latin "novellas" are not graded in the way textbook readers are: new words and structures do not rise chapter by chapter [UNVERIFIED in the registry; known from a search summary]. Muccigrosso (2004) makes the case for a frequency-based core vocabulary in a corpus language.

### c. Making comprehensible input when almost none exists

**Coverage.** Hu and Nation (2000) had learners read a story at 80%, 90%, 95% and 100% known-word coverage: none understood adequately at 80%, few at 90 to 95%, and 98% was their recommendation. Schmitt et al. (2011), with 661 readers, found a roughly linear relation between coverage and comprehension and no threshold. Webb et al. (2025) partly replicated Hu and Nation with 94 advanced learners: scores rose from 90% to 100%, but 90%, 95% and 98% did not differ reliably, and coverage explained 11% of the variance. Schmitt et al. (2017) had already warned that these thresholds rest on few studies.

**Repetition.** Webb (2007) found knowledge growing with each added encounter up to ten, without reaching full knowledge. In Waring and Takaki (2003), 15 learners read a 400-headword graded reader containing 25 disguised words; most were not learned, and on average one meaning in 25 was recalled after three months. The meta-analytic correlation between encounters and learning is r = .34 (26 studies, N = 1,918), larger with spacing and visual support (Uchihara et al. 2019). Nation (2014) calculated that natural text needs about 200,000 running words to give a dozen meetings of each second-thousand word, and about three million for the ninth thousand (figures recalled). Nation and Wang (1999) concluded from an analysis of a graded-reader series that learners need about a book a week and several books per level (recalled).

**Design of graded text.** Nation and Waring (2020) set out the standard scheme: fixed word lists per level, new words introduced sparingly, large quantities of easy reading. Simplified texts differ measurably from authentic ones (more cohesion, commoner words, simpler syntax), and writers who simplify by intuition produce those features (Crossley et al. 2007; Crossley et al. 2012). Narrow reading helps a small language: related texts recycle more vocabulary (Schmitt & Carter 2000), and learners read faster and understood more on a text related to a series they had read for three weeks (Chang & Millett 2017).

**Rule-generated input.** Grammar-driven generation exists as engineering. GramEx generates exercises from a grammar with controlled vocabulary and syntax (Perez-Beltrachini et al. 2012). MULLE generates Latin translation exercises from a Grammatical Framework grammar tied to a textbook's lessons (Lange & Ljunglöf 2018). De Lon and Listenmaa (2026) use one grammar for both presentation and answer checking. None reports learning outcomes. The nearest outcome evidence is from artificial-language experiments, where every sentence is produced by rule: adults reached high proficiency in a language of under twenty words through computer-based practice (Morgan-Short et al. 2012), and success in such tasks correlates with classroom language learning after controlling for IQ (Ettlinger et al. 2016). One finding matters for canon's inconsistencies: adults given inconsistent input reproduced the inconsistency, where children regularised it (Hudson Kam & Newport 2005).

### d. Curriculum sequencing (brief)

Frequency and range are the default order for vocabulary (Nation 2022); He and Godfroid (2019) add usefulness and difficulty. For grammar, the "natural order" is largely explained by salience, semantic complexity, regularity and frequency (Goldschneider & DeKeyser 2001), and its immunity to teaching is disputed (Zhang & Lantolf 2015). Dovahzul's word order is English and its morphology is a few suffixes (Track 1), so developmental-sequence research has little to say; order by dependency, frequency and transparency (inference, as Track 9). Spiral syllabi return to each item at greater depth (Martin 1978), which the spacing evidence supports (Kim & Webb 2022). Formulaic sequences lower processing load (Wray 2002); 16 child beginners unpacked rote chunks into productive grammar over two years (Myles et al. 1998); students made aware of chunks over 22 teaching hours were judged more proficient by blind raters (Boers et al. 2006; N = 32). The wider intervention record is mixed (Boers & Lindstromberg 2012). Backward design fits where outcomes can be stated first (Richards 2013; Nation & Macalister 2010), and can-do scales can be built empirically (North & Schneider 1998). Nation's (2007) four strands give the time budget.

### e. Assessment with no native-speaker norm (brief)

Cook (1999) argues the native speaker is the wrong yardstick for any learner; here there is none to use. Criterion-referenced testing measures against a defined domain (Brown & Hudson 2002), and for this language the domain can be enumerated completely: the lexicon, the ruled grammar, the text library. Fluency can be measured within the learner as speed and stability of processing (Segalowitz 2010). Self-assessment correlated .466 with performance across 97 samples, more with explicit criteria, training and reliable instruments (Li & Zhang 2021); Ross (1998) found it more accurate for skills learners had just practised. New-speaker research shows that legitimacy is anchored socially, in a record or community, not in birth (O'Rourke et al. 2015; Ó hIfearnáin 2015).

### f. Building a body of text

Every revival enlarged its corpus by translation and original writing under editorial control: Hebrew through schoolbooks, a press and committee word lists (Fellman 1973); Kaurna through songs, speeches and naming (Amery 2016); Esperanto through model texts (Fiedler 2006); Klingon and Na'vi through fan translation vetted by creators (Schreyer 2011). Translation is also effective study: 75 learners taught by contrastive analysis and translation outscored meaning-focused and other form-focused groups on vocabulary and collocation tests (Laufer & Girsai 2008). Composing pushes learners to notice gaps (Swain & Lapkin 1995). The risks are drift (Owens 2016), hybridisation toward the learners' first language (Zuckermann 2020) and frozen inconsistency (Hudson Kam & Newport 2005).

## 3. What this means for the app

### 3.1 What canon can and cannot supply

Computed from the non-duplicate word-wall, song, dialogue, shout and inscription records, names excluded (Track 9 counted 2,442 tokens under a narrower rule):

| Measure | Value |
|---|---|
| Running tokens / distinct forms | 2,590 / 746 |
| Forms occurring once | 348 (47%) |
| Forms occurring 6 or more / 10 or more times | 108 / 56 |
| Forms needed for 50% / 80% / 90% / 95% / 98% of tokens | 95 / 313 / 487 / 617 / 695 |
| Median words per line: word wall / dialogue | 4 / 2 |
| Of 521 multi-word canon lines, fully readable with the top 100 / 200 / 300 forms | 73 / 167 / 242 |
| Same, with at most one unknown word | 230 / 335 / 399 |
| Swadesh-207 / Leipzig-Jakarta-100 concepts with no entry | 81 / 41 |

Canon gives only 108 words the repetition the evidence asks for. It works as a goal ("you can now read 167 canon lines unaided"), not as input.

### 3.2 Curriculum architecture

Backward-designed from can-do goals (Richards 2013). Lemma counts and text volumes are design estimates. Volume starts from new lemmas x 12 meetings x 4, rounded up, where 4 is an assumed overhead for function words and recycled old words, to be calibrated from the library's own counts.

| Stage | Can-do goal | Cumulative lemmas | Rulings the forge must make first | Graded text that must exist |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| 0 Runes, sounds, formulas | Read and type the runes; say words by the project convention; use 30 fixed phrases and shout triads as chunks | about 40, inside chunks | G01 sounds, G04 rune spelling, G34 romanisation, G30 formulas | 30 formulas, shout phrases; about 300 words |
| 1 First sentences | Understand and build 3 to 6 word statements, commands, negation, possession | 100 | G06 pronouns, G07 possession, G09 tense, G13 copula, G12 negation, G14 articles, G08 plural, first verbs of G25 | about 5,000 words: 600 generated sentences, 15 micro-stories of 100 to 150 words |
| 2 Question and answer | Ask and answer; count; narrate; hold a scripted exchange | 300 | G11 questions, G05 numerals, rest of G25, G10 modality, G17 conjunctions | plus 12,000: two narrow-reading series of ten episodes, with question sets |
| 3 Reading canon | Read any word wall or song with glosses; write a wall-style inscription | about 650 (all attested canon) | G15, G16, G18, G21, G27 spelling, G28 glosses | plus 20,000: annotated canon reader and bridging stories |
| 4 Conversation and composition | Sustain roleplay turns; translate short prose | 1,000 to 1,200 (adds the missing core concepts) | G22 derivation, G23 compounding, G19, G20, G33 | plus 25,000, including validated community texts |
| 5 Forge contributor | Coin by rule; review submissions | open | G26, G29, G31, G32 | grows by submission |

All seven blocking gaps fall in Stages 0 to 2, so those rulings come before any content. The total through Stage 4 is about 62,000 running words, some 24 times the canon corpus. Confidence: medium for the ordering, low for the volumes.

### 3.3 Generating and assembling graded input

1. **Stage word lists as data.** Each lemma record: forms, class, gloss, tier (canon, semi-canon, forge), stage, prerequisites. Confidence high (Nation & Waring 2020).
2. **Unlock ordering.** Within a stage, order lemmas greedily by how many canon lines each makes readable with at most one unknown word, ties broken by corpus frequency. Confidence medium (inference from frequency ordering and the line counts above).
3. **One grammar, two outputs.** Store each sentence pattern as an abstract tree with typed slots (agent, creature, place, quality), and render it both as Dovahzul and as English. The same tree checks learner answers (De Lon & Listenmaa 2026; Lange & Ljunglöf 2018). Slot typing keeps output meaningful. Confidence medium as engineering, low as pedagogy.
4. **Use generated sentences where form decides meaning**: picture or gloss matching, either-or and yes-no question sets over a story, substitution drills. Confidence medium (Track 9; Stafford et al. 2012).
5. **Hand-written stories, machine-linted.** The linter rejects a text unless: every lemma is at or below the text's stage; known-word coverage is at least 98% (95% if every new word is glossed); new lemmas number at most one per 50 running words, or five in a text under 150 words; each new lemma occurs at least three times in its first text. Confidence medium; thresholds soft (Webb et al. 2025).
6. **Recycling ledger.** Count each lemma's occurrences across the next eight texts of its series; flag any below ten. The forge dashboard lists under-recycled lemmas as writing requests. Confidence medium (Webb 2007; Uchihara et al. 2019).
7. **Series, not singles.** Fixed cast, setting and topic for ten episodes (Schmitt & Carter 2000; Chang & Millett 2017). Confidence medium.
8. **Provenance on every line**: canon, ruled, generated or community. Confidence high as policy (Owens 2016; Ó hIfearnáin 2015).
9. **Resolve variation before teaching it.** Where canon is inconsistent (possessive suffixes, optional plural), teach one ruled form and show the others as attested variants (Hudson Kam & Newport 2005; inference).

### 3.4 Assessment

- Gate each stage on a criterion: for example 90% on a sampled item bank per skill, with skills tracked separately (Brown & Hudson 2002). The 90% figure is a starting value.
- Report fluency as the learner's own reading rate on 98%-coverage text and response-time stability, compared with their past values (Segalowitz 2010).
- Use can-do checklists immediately after the matching task, with explicit criteria. Do not gate on them (Li & Zhang 2021; Ross 1998).
- Never display "native-like". Display what can be read and done.

### 3.5 Community text as curriculum

From Stage 3, translation and composition tasks feed a submission queue. A submission must parse under the ruled grammar, use only approved lemmas, and carry a computed stage and coverage profile; the owner approves. Settle one normative spelling (G27, G34) before opening the queue: Cornish shows the cost of delay (Sayers 2012). Gap-filling requests follow the ladder in section 2a, internal resources first. Confidence medium: this is the pattern of every documented case, but none measured its effect on learning.

## 4. Contested or weak evidence

- **Latin and Greek method comparisons:** no controlled study found. Claims for the nature method rest on practitioner reports.
- **Coverage thresholds:** 98% comes from one study of 66 readers; the replication did not separate 90% from 98%.
- **Reclamation:** case studies written largely by participants; speaker numbers and proficiency are seldom measured. Hebrew's conditions are not reproducible.
- **Generated text as input:** no outcome study. Artificial-language experiments use tiny lexicons and hours of training; extending them to a thousand-word language is extrapolation.
- **Formulaic teaching:** one small positive study, a mixed review.
- **Story-based teaching research:** small and largely by advocates.
- **Text-volume estimates in 3.2** are arithmetic from repetition studies of natural languages, not measurements.
