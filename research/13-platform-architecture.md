# 13. Platform and architecture

Written 2026-11-14. This track rests on platform documentation; peer-reviewed sources are used where they exist. **NRV** marks a platform fact stated from the author's knowledge of the documentation and **not re-verified by search in this session**: test it before relying on it. Sub-questions a, c, f and g are treated in depth, b and e moderately, d and h briefly.

## 1. Bottom line

- **Build one static, offline-first installable web app and treat it as the product; wrappers are packaging.** It is the only route that costs nothing, needs no signing, and does not care which chip the owner's MacBook has.
- **Unsigned native installers are now the high-friction path on all three systems.** macOS 15 and later send the user into System Settings; Windows shows a SmartScreen block; certified Android devices are moving to verified developers only (four countries since September 2026, global from 2027).
- **Free ways round the warnings exist for Windows and Android, not for macOS.** Microsoft Store registration is free; F-Droid is free; Apple notarisation costs 99 US dollars a year with no free tier.
- **Wrapper: Tauri 2 for desktop installers, not Electron** (megabytes against roughly a hundred, and it also targets Android). Use Capacitor for Android only if a Tauri Android spike fails.
- **One rule engine, in pure TypeScript with rules as versioned JSON, runs in the app and in continuous integration.** CI always takes the engine and rules from the protected main branch, never from the contribution.
- **Contribution flow: local validation, then a prefilled issue or an exported file, then a CI verdict, then the owner's decision, then a signed lexicon pack.** Automatic validation filters; it never accepts.
- **Rule-valid words can be mass-generated by script, so cap volume per contributor** and keep the English gloss, the only free-text field, under human review.
- **Sign lexicon packs with Ed25519:** an offline owner key pinned in the app, delegating a short-lived CI key.

## 2. Findings by sub-question

### a. Installable web apps

**Install behaviour.**
- Android: Chrome installs a web app as a real package that Google's service builds and signs; Firefox only adds a shortcut (NRV).
- macOS: Safari 17 on macOS 14 or later offers File > Add to Dock for any site. The Dock app keeps storage separate from Safari and gets the browser-level quota, about 60% of disk (MDN n.d.; WebKit 2023). Chrome and Edge install from the address bar; Firefox on macOS has no install feature (NRV).
- Windows: Edge is preinstalled and installs web apps to Start and the taskbar; so does Chrome (NRV).

**Storage and eviction.** Chromium allows an origin up to about 60% of disk and Firefox the smaller of 10% of disk or 10 GiB in best-effort mode (MDN n.d.; these two figures NRV); Safari allows about 60% for the browser and Dock apps and 15% for web views inside other apps (WebKit 2023). The whole app with audio should stay under 100 MB (estimate), so quota is not the constraint. Eviction is: best-effort storage is cleared least-recently-used under disk pressure, and Safari deletes script-written storage of sites unused for seven days of browser use, which installed web apps escape (NRV). `navigator.storage.persist()` asks for exemption; the browser decides.

**Caching.** The right pattern for a versioned offline app is a precached shell served cache-first, with file names carrying content hashes. Precache installation is all-or-nothing, so a large audio set does not belong in it. In the one controlled study found, service workers had no significant effect on energy use across seven web apps and two devices (Malavolta et al. 2017; small sample).

**One online visit is unavoidable** for the web app: the service worker and content arrive over HTTPS on first load. A Safari Dock app probably needs its own first launch online because its storage is separate (NRV; test).

**Updating.** The browser re-checks the service-worker script on navigation; a new version installs in the background and waits. The app should prompt and never reload mid-lesson.

### c. Costs and friction at zero budget

**macOS.** Avoiding Gatekeeper needs a Developer ID signature plus notarisation, which needs the paid programme at 99 US dollars a year; signing without notarisation is blocked like an unsigned app (Copes n.d.). For an unnotarised app on macOS 15 or later the old Control-click shortcut is gone: the user must try to open it, dismiss the block, go to System Settings > Privacy & Security, click Open Anyway, confirm and authenticate (Apple n.d.; Oakley 2024). Apple-chip binaries must at least be ad-hoc signed to run at all (NRV).

**Windows.** An unsigned installer triggers "Windows protected your PC". Certificates no longer buy instant trust: Microsoft's guidance says extended-validation certificates do not bypass SmartScreen, reputation accrues over time, and its own signing service costs about 10 US dollars a month (Microsoft n.d. a; Microsoft n.d. b; Lawrence 2024). Smart App Control, where enforcing, blocks unknown unsigned apps with no override (NRV). Store apps are re-signed by Microsoft and show no SmartScreen prompt; registration is free for individuals since September 2025 and for companies since May 2026, with identity verification (Microsoft 2025; Microsoft 2026).

**Android.** Google's developer verification requires apps on certified devices to be registered to a verified developer. It took effect in Brazil, Indonesia, Singapore and Thailand in September 2026, with global rollout from 2027 (Google n.d. a; Pinggy 2026). A full account costs 25 US dollars and needs identity documents; a limited-distribution account is free but capped at 20 devices; an "advanced flow" lets a user allow unverified apps after a one-time setup; ADB installs are unaffected (Google 2026; Google n.d. b). F-Droid is free and builds from source, but signs most apps with its own key; it opposes the scheme, and how its builds will satisfy it is unresolved (F-Droid 2026; Pinggy 2026, a secondary source). The main store additionally requires new personal accounts to run a closed test with 12 testers for 14 days (NRV). Inference: a web app installed through Chrome is packaged by Google's service, not distributed by the developer, so verification should not touch it; no source read states this.

**What unsigned means for ordinary users.** A redesigned browser warning led nearly 30% more users to stay safe, yet comprehension stayed low (Felt et al. 2015). Inference: behaviour follows a warning's design more than its meaning, so many ordinary users will stop at an operating-system block, and written bypass instructions train a habit attackers exploit. Signatures attest identity, not safety; validly signed malware is documented (Kim et al. 2017). Checksums, build provenance (Newman et al. 2022) and reproducible builds (Lamb and Zacchiroli 2022) help expert verifiers, not ordinary users (inference).

### d. Hosting (brief)

GitHub Pages serves a public repository free over HTTPS: site at most 1 GB, soft limit 100 GB of bandwidth a month, 10-minute deployment limit (GitHub n.d.). At 30 MB per full install that is about 3,300 new installs a month. Release pages host installers and pack archives free (NRV on limits). Two traps, both NRV: Pages cannot set custom response headers, and release downloads are not readable by cross-origin `fetch`, so in-app pack updates must come from the app's own origin.

### e. Stack (see section 3)

Two facts come from the repository. The 657 canon entries reduced to word, definitions and part of speech are 25 KB of JSON, 5.7 KB gzipped (measured on `research/data/canon-lexicon.json`), so several thousand full entries fit in memory. And `tools/analysis/phonotactics.py` says its rules are "meant to be simple enough to re-implement in the app"; its `forge_profile` and lexicon syllabification can serve as golden test data.

### f. Serverless contribution pipeline

**Moderation load.** When Wikipedia's main anti-vandalism bot went down, damage was still reverted but took roughly twice as long (Geiger and Halfaker 2013): automation buys speed, people remain the backstop. Automated and impersonal rejection of good-faith newcomers accompanied Wikipedia's fall in retention (Halfaker et al. 2013). A systematic review lists orientation, technical set-up and reception as the main newcomer barriers in open source (Steinmacher et al. 2015). Hostile exchanges on issue trackers are a documented strain on maintainers (Raman et al. 2020; exploratory, small sample).

**Abuse surface.** Workflows triggered by untrusted text are a known attack class in taxonomies of supply-chain attacks, and weak permissions and injection are common in real workflow files (Ladisa et al. 2023; Benedetti et al. 2022). Project-specific risks (inference):
1. flooding with scripted rule-valid words;
2. offensive or defamatory glosses;
3. licence laundering, such as bulk copying from fan dictionaries;
4. a pull request that edits the rules or engine so its own word passes;
5. tampered packs on mirrors or passed by file.

**Update security.** Survivable update systems separate signing roles, keep the most trusted keys offline and let online keys expire (Samuel et al. 2010). Ed25519 gives 32-byte keys and 64-byte signatures (Bernstein et al. 2012).

### g. Data model

The FAIR principles ask for globally unique persistent identifiers, a clear licence and detailed provenance on every record (Wilkinson et al. 2016). About a third of Maven library releases contained breaking changes whatever their version number said (Raemaekers et al. 2017), so version discipline must be machine-checked. The repository's data carries UESP's Attribution-ShareAlike 2.5 licence and its README warns that most English glosses are wiki editors' readings, so the form of a word and its gloss have different authorities and need separate provenance.

### h. Local-first principles (brief)

Kleppmann et al. (2019) set seven ideals: fast, multi-device, offline, collaborative, long-lived, private, user-controlled. It is a position paper with prototypes, not a controlled evaluation. Conflict-free replicated data types converge whatever the merge order; the simplest is a grow-only set (Shapiro et al. 2011). Making merges safe against malicious peers needs hash-linked operations (Kleppmann 2022). Inference: this app needs no general replication, because study progress is single-user and the lexicon has one authority. An installed, exportable, open-format app meets the offline, longevity, privacy and control ideals by construction; multi-device is met by file export; collaboration is deliberately asynchronous and curated.

## 3. What this means for the app

**Recommended architecture.** A monorepo: `packages/engine` (pure TypeScript), `rules/` (JSON), `lexicon/` (JSON), `app/` (web app), `wrappers/` (later).

1. **Stack.** TypeScript in strict mode; Vite; Svelte 5 as a single-page app (Preact is equivalent; engine and data stay framework-free); `vite-plugin-pwa` with Workbox. *Confidence: medium; convention, not evidence.*
2. **Two-tier offline cache.** Precache only the shell, fonts and lexicon (target under 3 MB). Fetch audio through an app-managed downloader into Cache Storage, per file with SHA-256 checks and a visible "offline ready" percentage. *Evidence: atomic precache; quotas (MDN n.d.). Confidence: high.*
3. **Progress storage.** IndexedDB behind a `ProgressStore` interface. Store an append-only log of review events, each with a UUID; derive scheduler state from it. Call `navigator.storage.persist()` after the first completed lesson and show the result. Offer export and import of one JSON file; import merges by set union of event IDs. *Evidence: Shapiro et al. 2011; Kleppmann et al. 2019. Confidence: high.*
4. **Lexicon delivery.** One JSON pack loaded into memory with a `Map` index; no database. Audio as one short mono clip per entry (about 24 MB for 5,000 clips at 32 kbit/s, estimate); choose the codec by a playback test in all three engines. The script font must be the project's own drawing under an open licence, since the data holds no rune shapes. *Evidence: measured lexicon size (2e). Confidence: medium.*
5. **Rule engine.** `validate(candidate, ruleset, lexicon)` returns a deterministic report listing each rule ID with pass or fail. No DOM, no clock, no randomness. The app and CI import the same package version. *Confidence: high.*
6. **Tests.** Vitest; golden tests requiring every canon word to pass or sit on an explicit exception list, and the syllabifier to reproduce the Python output; property-based tests for determinism and round-tripping (Claessen and Hughes 2000); Playwright in Chromium, WebKit and Firefox, including a run with the network off after install. *Confidence: high.*
7. **Submission format.** Canonical JSON: schema version, ruleset version and hash, engine version, the entry, its derivation, the report hash, a licence declaration. Submission ID is `sub-` plus the first 16 base32 characters of its SHA-256, so duplicates collapse. *Evidence: content-hash identifiers, by analogy with Kleppmann 2022. Confidence: medium.*
8. **Three export routes.** (i) A prefilled issue link using an issue form; keep the payload under about 6,000 characters (NRV on the URL limit). (ii) A downloadable file. (iii) Without an account: the file goes by email or any community channel to a volunteer sponsor, whose import script validates it and opens a batch pull request recording `submitted_via` and the contributor's chosen handle. An anonymous relay function is rejected for now: it would hold a secret and be the main spam target. *Confidence: medium.*
9. **CI validation.** On issue open or edit: pass the body through an environment variable, never into a shell line; cap its size; run the engine at the pinned ruleset; check collisions and near-duplicates; post a report naming each failed rule and its fix; label `valid`, `invalid` or `duplicate`. Token permissions: issues write, contents read. For pull requests use the `pull_request` trigger, take engine and rules from main, read only data files from the contribution, and fail outside changes to anything but `lexicon/submissions/`. Pin third-party actions by commit. *Evidence: Ladisa et al. 2023; Benedetti et al. 2022; Halfaker et al. 2013. Confidence: high.*
10. **Volume limits.** Start at 5 open submissions and 20 per 30 days per author, closing the excess with an explanation; a weekly digest issue lists valid pending entries for batch decision. *Confidence: low on the numbers; tune.*
11. **Owner authority.** Owner is the only account with write access. A branch ruleset on main requires pull requests and passing checks and forbids force pushes; CODEOWNERS assigns `rules/`, `packages/engine/`, `.github/` and `keys/` to the owner; a tag ruleset protects `rules-v*` and `lexicon-v*` (NRV that all are available on a free public repository). Acceptance is the owner applying an `accepted` label, which triggers a bot pull request the owner merges. *Confidence: medium until the NRV is checked.*
12. **Signed packs.** Two owner root public keys pinned in the app, private halves offline. A root-signed `keys.json` delegates a CI release key for at most 90 days. CI signs a manifest: pack version (monotonic, this report's own rollback guard), ruleset version, SHA-256, size, minimum app version. The app verifies signature, hash and that the version is higher, swaps atomically, and keeps the previous pack. Expiry stops new updates only; it never disables an installed pack. Trade-off: in the hosted web app, code and key share an origin, so signatures chiefly protect file-passed packs, mirrors and wrappers. *Evidence: Samuel et al. 2010. Confidence: medium-high.*
13. **Entry model.**

```json
{"id":"DZ-000123","key":"aak","lemma":"Aak","pos":null,
 "senses":[{"gloss":"Guide","gloss_authority":"wiki-editor"}],
 "tier":"canon","status":"active",
 "provenance":{"form_authority":"game","source":"UESP Lore:Dragon Language","revid":3619598,"refs":["Paarthurnax"]},
 "derivation":null,
 "validated_under":null,
 "licence":{"form":"LicenseRef-game-text","gloss":"CC-BY-SA-2.5","audio":null},
 "superseded_by":null}
```

   - Tiers: `canon` (game or official text, flagged canon or semi-canon), `community` (fan coinages from outside the forge), `forged-pending` (engine-valid, undecided), `forged-accepted` (owner-accepted, in a signed pack).
   - CI assigns permanent `DZ-` numbers at import or acceptance. The tier is a field, so promotion keeps the ID; entries are deprecated, never deleted.
   - Forged entries fill `derivation` (process, input entry IDs, rule IDs) and `validated_under` (ruleset version and hash).
   - New contributions are declared CC BY-SA 4.0 by a required checkbox. *Evidence: Wilkinson et al. 2016. Confidence: high on structure, low on licence labels; the legal track must rule.*
14. **Ruleset versioning.** Semantic version plus SHA-256 of the canonical JSON. CI computes the required bump by running old and new rules over canon, accepted entries and a fixed fuzz corpus: any accepted entry newly failing means major; only new passes means minor; identical verdicts means patch. On a major bump, accepted entries are kept and flagged, and the owner decides. *Evidence: Raemaekers et al. 2017. Confidence: medium-high.*

**Staged delivery.**
1. **First usable release:** the web app on GitHub Pages with lessons, forge, local validation, file and issue export, progress backup and install guides. No cost, no warnings.
2. **Contribution pipeline:** CI validation, protections, signed packs, in-app pack update.
3. **Desktop installers,** Tauri-built on hosted runners: a universal macOS disk image (ad-hoc signed, with honest Open Anyway instructions) and a Windows installer on the releases page, plus a free Microsoft Store listing, which removes the Windows warning.
4. **Android package:** F-Droid submission and a direct download, after checking where verification stands; the 25-dollar verified account is the first money worth spending.
5. **Only if donations arrive:** Apple notarisation.

**Trade-offs accepted.** One online visit until stage 3; no automatic multi-device sync; a multi-step approval for Mac installer users; a sponsor for contributors without an account.

## 4. Contested or weak evidence

- Peer-reviewed work on installable web apps dates from 2017 and 2018 and uses toy apps or conceptual comparison (Biørn-Hansen et al. 2017; Majchrzak et al. 2018; Biørn-Hansen et al. 2018); it supports feasibility, nothing about 2026 platform policy.
- Size comparisons between wrappers come from vendor and blog benchmarks, not reproducible studies.
- Android verification: sources disagree on scope and dates, the live state of the advanced flow in November 2026 is unverified, and F-Droid's position under it is unresolved.
- Wording of the macOS 26 block dialog differs between sources.
- Warning research concerns browser certificate warnings; applying it to installer blocks is inference.
- Wikipedia findings come from a very large wiki; transfer to a small project is by analogy.
- Rate limits, cache sizes and key lifetimes above are engineering starting points, not evidence-derived.

## 5. Gaps and blocked items

- Page fetching was unavailable, so documentation was read only through search summaries. Every NRV item needs a direct check. Also unverified because no search covered them: hosted-runner labels and free allowances for macOS builds; individual eligibility for Microsoft's signing service; free signing foundations for open source; Ed25519 support in Web Crypto.
- No build spike was run. Tauri on Android and a universal macOS build on a hosted runner must each be proven before stage 3.
- The data contains no rune shapes, no pronunciation guide and no audio; the source and licence of fonts and recordings are open.
- Shipping game-derived words, and whether F-Droid would flag them as non-free assets, are legal questions for another track.
- No search budget remained for alternative hosts or for post-2022 research on moderating community lexicons.
